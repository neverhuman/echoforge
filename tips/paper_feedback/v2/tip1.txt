I reviewed the uploaded EchoForge IEEE draft in detail, including the rendered pages and the parsed content. My overall view: **the paper has a strong and defensible benchmark concept, but it currently reads more like an evidence-ledger/report than a polished radar paper.** The best ideas are absolutely worth keeping: the strict synthetic claim boundary, group-locked holdout, feature denylisting, low-FPR KPI, phase breakdown, false-alarm-family breakdown, ablations, and compare-only measured anchors. The biggest weaknesses are: **insufficient radar-model specificity, very small positive holdout support, confusing metric/table consistency, opaque component names, and several figures/tables that are not publication-ready.** 

## Executive verdict

The paper does a very good job protecting itself from overclaiming. The abstract and first section repeatedly state that the benchmark is synthetic public-proxy evidence, not measured-platform truth or operational field performance; the title/abstract also clearly identify the positive class as a fixed-wing pusher-prop public proxy and state the main corpus scale of 10,000 scenario groups, 50 positive groups, and 30,000 phase records. 

However, a radar reviewer will likely ask: **“What exactly is being simulated, how close is the signal chain to a physically meaningful radar chain, how much uncertainty is due to only 8 positive holdout groups, and why should I trust the advanced fusion improvement?”** Right now, the answer is partially there, but scattered across narrative, tables, and cramped figures. The paper needs a stronger bridge between **radar physics**, **synthetic generation**, and **detector evaluation**.

My recommended priority order before submission:

1. **Fix the figures first**, especially Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 5, Fig. 6, and Fig. 8. Several are too small, overlapped, or visually nonstandard.
2. **Fix Table IV / KPI consistency.** The paper says the primary KPI is LCB95 Recall@1%FPR, but Table IV reports AP confidence intervals, not the primary KPI interval. Also, the TP/FP/TN/FN counts do not match the listed 1% FPR recall threshold, so the table needs clearer labeling. 
3. **Rebuild Table V completely.** It is currently the weakest table: aliases are hard to parse, there are apparent string/typo issues such as “degeodesic” and “godesic,” and the weights should be sorted and visually grouped. 
4. **Add radar-specific technical detail**: Doppler resolution, velocity resolution, chirp/FMCW assumptions, SNR/noise model, clutter distributions, RCS/aspect priors, prop/micro-Doppler parameter priors, and how 6 ms CPI relates to 30–150 s phase windows.
5. **Temper the claims around robustness** because the holdout contains only 24 positive records and 8 positive groups, and the false-alarm table has only one false alarm. 
6. **Use standard radar/ML baseline names** rather than only internal fusion labels. Add CA-CFAR/OS-CFAR-style detector baselines, simple micro-Doppler feature baselines, and at least one conventional ML baseline.

# What the paper does well

## 1. The claim boundary is unusually strong

This is one of the best parts of the draft. The paper explicitly avoids measured-platform truth claims and repeatedly states that the benchmark is synthetic public-proxy evidence, not field-performance equivalence. That is the right posture for a synthetic radar benchmark. The abstract, Section I, validation-tier table, compare-only anchor discussion, limitations, and conclusion all reinforce this boundary.   

The good part: reviewers cannot easily accuse the paper of pretending to be a measured C-UAS trial. You are very clear that the benchmark is for reviewable synthetic evidence under declared assumptions.

The gap: the paper says “strict-open” many times, but it does not define that phrase as a formal artifact policy. You should define it once, early, as something like:

> **Strict-open** means that all detector-facing products, generation scripts, seeds, split manifests, model cards, evaluation scripts, metric traces, and audit artifacts are public/reproducible, while proprietary or measured raw files are excluded and no named-platform truth is claimed.

That would make “strict-open” feel like an engineering standard rather than a slogan.

## 2. Group-locked splitting is exactly the right idea

The split policy is a strong contribution. The paper says each scenario group expands into three locked windows—initial take-up, climb transition, and cruise altitude—and split assignment happens at the scenario-group level so all phase records from one group share the same split role. This is exactly the right approach because row-level splitting would leak group identity through phase windows. 

This is a genuinely important benchmark-design point. Keep it prominent. I would make it even more visual and explicit: one group, three phase rows, one split assignment. A reviewer should understand the leakage prevention in 10 seconds.

## 3. The low-FPR KPI is appropriate for radar

The paper chooses LCB95 Recall@1%FPR as the headline KPI because the benchmark is rare-positive, false-alarm constrained, and group-correlated. That is directionally correct for low-altitude UAS radar. ROC AUC alone is not operationally meaningful when the relevant region is low FPR. 

The gap is execution: the primary KPI is not displayed cleanly as the primary result. It is described in Section IV/Table III, but the main summary table reports AP confidence intervals instead. The primary KPI should appear in the main results table with its point estimate and group-block bootstrap interval.

## 4. Phase-wise behavior is valuable

Table VIII and Fig. 4 show that the initial take-up phase is harder, climb transition improves, and cruise is most separable. That is exactly the kind of diagnostic a radar reviewer wants. It maps performance to an operational sequence rather than treating the dataset as an unstructured classification problem. 

The gap: with only 8 positives per phase in holdout, the phase claims need confidence intervals or at least “n=8 positives per phase” visually emphasized.

## 5. False-alarm-family breakdown is the right diagnostic

The family breakdown is conceptually excellent. A radar system paper should not just say “one false alarm”; it should say whether failures are birds, RC fixed-wing, weather, RFI, ground vehicles, clutter-only counterfactuals, wind turbines, multipath ghosts, etc. Table IX reports that the only locked-candidate false alarm is in `single_bird`. 

The gap: because there is only one false alarm, the paper should not overinterpret this as a stable robustness pattern. It is a useful diagnostic, not a statistically strong family-level conclusion.

# Major technical gaps from a radar-industry perspective

## 1. The radar signal chain needs more physical specificity

The generative model starts with a radar-equation-style received-power proxy and a complex IQ equation with target, clutter, interference, and receiver impairment terms. That is a good skeleton.  But for radar reviewers, it is not enough.

You should add the following details:

**Waveform clarity.** Fig. 1 says “multibranch FMCW-style public-proxy radar,” but Table I uses PRF/CPI/pulse language. That can be fine, but you need to state whether “pulse” means chirp, sweep, pulse repetition interval, or a generic coherent sample. FMCW radar usually needs chirp slope, sweep bandwidth, chirp duration, ADC sampling, beat-frequency mapping, and range FFT assumptions. If you keep “PRF/CPI,” define it as chirp repetition frequency and coherent processing interval for FMCW-like processing.

**Derived radar resolutions.** Table I correctly implies range resolutions of 0.25 m for 600 MHz, 0.83 m for 180 MHz, and 0.50 m for 300 MHz. But it does not show Doppler resolution or velocity resolution. With a 6 ms CPI, Doppler-bin spacing is about 167 Hz. At X/Ku this corresponds to relatively coarse velocity resolution for detailed micro-Doppler discrimination. If the classifier uses phase windows of 0–30 s, 30–90 s, and 90–150 s, explain how many CPIs are aggregated and how micro-Doppler features are estimated over those longer windows.

**RCS priors.** The paper says the RCS term is aspect- and frequency-dependent, but it does not define the prior family. A radar reviewer will want at least a public-proxy RCS model card: median RCS range, aspect lobes, Swerling-like fluctuation model, propeller modulation terms, body/wing contributions, and uncertainty bounds.

**Clutter model.** Low-altitude UAS detection is dominated by clutter and hard negatives. You mention clutter, multipath, terrain glint, weather, wind turbine, and RFI, but not the distributions. Add whether clutter amplitude is Rayleigh, Weibull, K-distributed, lognormal, compound-Gaussian, or empirical-proxy. Add grazing-angle and land-cover dependence.

**Noise/SNR model.** The model card should include thermal noise assumptions, receiver noise figure or proxy equivalent, SNR range, dynamic range, AGC behavior, quantization depth, and clipping/compression behavior.

**Micro-Doppler physics.** For fixed-wing pusher-prop UAS, propeller blade count, RPM range, blade-tip velocity, modulation bandwidth, blade-flash intermittency, and aspect dependence matter. Even if synthetic, you should declare priors and uncertainty ranges.

## 2. The positive class is too small for confident low-FPR claims

The main run has 10,000 scenario groups but only 50 positive groups, and the holdout contains 1,500 groups with 8 positive groups, corresponding to 24 positive phase records.  

That is a severe statistical limitation. It is acceptable for a stress-test benchmark, but the paper must treat the result as preliminary. At 24 positive holdout records, every missed positive changes recall by 4.17 percentage points. At 8 positive groups, every missed group is even more consequential for group-block bootstrap uncertainty.

Recommended fix: add one of the following:

* A larger positive holdout, ideally at least 50–100 positive groups.
* A multi-seed evaluation with several independently generated corpora.
* A rare-positive justification that explicitly says the benchmark is testing false-alarm control under scarcity, not estimating field Pd with tight confidence.
* A table of uncertainty at the **group** level, not just the record level.

## 3. The “improves ranking” statement needs qualification

Table IV shows the locked advanced candidate has much higher AP and much higher 1% FPR recall than the layered fusion baseline. That is a strong result: AP 0.824845 versus 0.128188, and 1% FPR recall 0.833333 versus 0.291667. But Table IV also shows the locked candidate has **lower ROC AUC** than the baseline: 0.916564 versus 0.937723. 

So the paper should not broadly say it improves “ranking” without qualification. Better wording:

> The locked candidate substantially improves precision-recall ranking and low-FPR operating behavior, while ROC AUC is slightly lower than the layered fusion baseline; this supports the choice of AP and low-FPR recall as more relevant metrics for the rare-positive operating regime.

That is honest and technically stronger.

## 4. Table IV has a threshold/count mismatch that must be clarified

From Table IV, the locked candidate has TP=18 and FN=6, so the operating-point recall from those counts is 18/24 = 0.75. But the table’s “1% FPR recall” column says 0.833333. Similarly, the baseline has TP=10 and FN=14, so the count-based recall is 0.4167, while the “1% FPR recall” column says 0.291667. Also, the baseline FP count is 49 with TN=4427, which is an FPR of about 1.095%, slightly above 1%. 

This may be because the TP/FP/TN/FN counts are from a selected threshold, while the 1% FPR recall is from a separate threshold sweep. That is fine, but the table must say so. Suggested columns:

| Method | Threshold type | TP | FP | TN | FN | FPR | Recall at threshold | Recall@≤1%FPR | AP | ROC AUC | LCB95 Recall@≤1%FPR |
| ------ | -------------: | -: | -: | -: | -: | --: | ------------------: | ------------: | -: | ------: | ------------------: |

This is an important fix. Reviewers notice threshold inconsistencies immediately.

## 5. The measured-anchor comparison needs normalization

The paper uses KTH as a compare-only measured anchor and explicitly says it is not transferring truth to the synthetic fixed-wing class. That is good.  

But KTH is a 77 GHz FMCW dataset, while EchoForge includes X/Ku, S-band, and GBAD-like branches. Direct comparison of micro-Doppler bandwidth in Hz is not generally fair across carrier frequencies because Doppler frequency scales with carrier. For compare-only shape checks, normalize Doppler to radial velocity or dimensionless modulation statistics. Fig. 8 currently does not make this clear.

Also, the Han/Jung 2026 time-synchronized multi-sensor dataset appears to be a real Scientific Data article with FMCW radar, CW radar, and RF receiver, so it could be a useful future multimodal anchor; if the paper says “pending license review,” then describe it as “candidate anchor, not used in current results” rather than implying it has already been used. ([Nature][1])

# Section-by-section feedback

## Title

Current title:

> **EchoForge: A Strict-Open Synthetic Benchmark for Low-Altitude UAS Radar and Multimodal Sensing**

This is strong and clear. It communicates the benchmark nature, synthetic nature, UAS domain, and multimodal angle. The issue is that “strict-open” is not a standard term. That is acceptable if you define it sharply.

Suggested alternatives:

> **EchoForge: A Strict-Open Public-Proxy Benchmark for Low-Altitude UAS Radar and Multimodal Detection**

or:

> **EchoForge: A Reproducible Synthetic Benchmark for Low-Altitude UAS Radar Under Explicit Public-Proxy Assumptions**

The first is better if “strict-open” is central to the contribution. The second is better if targeting conservative radar reviewers.

## Abstract

What it does well: it is honest, specific, and bounded. It states the positive class, the corpus size, the group-locked split, the blind holdout, and the fact that the goal is reviewable synthetic evidence rather than field-performance equivalence. 

What to improve:

Add one sentence with headline results. The abstract currently says you report ranking, operating-point, calibration, and ablation diagnostics, but it does not give the result. A technical abstract should contain at least one quantitative outcome.

Suggested replacement ending:

> On the blind holdout, the locked candidate improves AP from 0.128 to 0.825 and Recall@≤1%FPR from 0.292 to 0.833 relative to the prior layered-fusion baseline, with group-block uncertainty reported because the holdout contains only 8 positive scenario groups. The result is synthetic public-proxy evidence under declared assumptions, not measured-platform truth or operational field performance.

That one sentence both improves impact and avoids overclaiming.

## Section I: Claim Boundary and Contributions

This section is conceptually strong. It explains that EchoForge is intentionally narrower than measured radar collection, publishes detector-facing products with uncertainty and validation tiers, and avoids measured-platform, proprietary-equivalent, or classified-fidelity claims. 

Main improvement: reduce repetition. The claim boundary appears in the abstract, Section I, Table II, anchor discussion, limitations, and conclusion. That is safe, but it becomes heavy. Keep the strongest version in Section I and refer back to it later.

Suggested structure:

1. Problem: low-altitude UAS radar benchmarks need reproducible hard-negative evaluation.
2. Constraint: measured collections are expensive, restricted, and not always open.
3. EchoForge answer: strict-open synthetic public-proxy benchmark.
4. Contributions:

   * signal-chain model card;
   * group-locked split and denylist;
   * low-FPR holdout evaluation;
   * calibration/ablation/false-alarm diagnostics;
   * compare-only measured anchors.

Avoid wording like “substantive rather than cosmetic.” It sounds defensive. The contributions are already substantive; let the list prove that.

## Section II: Radar and Multimodal Generative Model

This is the section that most needs technical strengthening. The received-power proxy and complex IQ equation are good starts, and Table I’s branch assumptions are useful. 

Add the following:

**A radar-parameter derivation paragraph.** After Table I, explicitly derive range resolution and Doppler resolution:

* ΔR = c / 2B.
* ΔfD ≈ 1 / TCPI.
* Δv ≈ λ / 2TCPI for monostatic radar.

This will reassure radar readers that the model card is physically coherent.

**A waveform paragraph.** Clarify whether the system is FMCW, pulsed-Doppler, or an abstract range-Doppler generator. Right now, “FMCW-style” and “PRF/pulses” coexist without enough explanation.

**A target-prior paragraph.** Describe public-proxy fixed-wing pusher-prop priors: body RCS, wing aspect effects, propeller RPM, blade count, micro-Doppler modulation, radial velocity ranges, altitude/range/aspect distributions.

**A clutter/impairment paragraph.** The receiver impairment list is useful—AGC compression, clock drift, quantization, dropped CPIs, PRF ambiguity, Doppler folding, calibration offset, multipath masking—but each needs at least one sentence or a table row describing how it is parameterized. 

**Multimodal detail.** Acoustic and passive-RF cue definitions are currently too abstract. Add node count, sampling/feature windows, cue families, SNR/noise priors, synchronization assumptions, and missing-data behavior.

## Section III: Scenario Design and Detector Views

This section has a strong design idea: group-level splits and detector-view restriction. Keep that. The detector-facing views are intentionally narrower than generator state, preventing labels, split keys, and hidden generator metadata from becoming shortcuts. 

Needed improvements:

**Show a scenario-card table.** Include rows for range, altitude, aspect, radial velocity, clutter type, site type, weather, RFI, hard-negative family, and phase window.

**State the positive rate prominently.** Fifty positive groups out of 10,000 is 0.5% at group level. That is central to the benchmark and should be openly framed.

**Clarify targeted holdout.** The paper mentions default group-random holdout and targeted holdout support for diagnostic OOD slices. Add either results for a targeted holdout or move that claim to future work.

**Detector-view denylist.** Add a mini-table listing allowed feature families and forbidden fields. Do not leave denylisting only as prose.

## Section IV: Evaluation Protocol and Calibration

This section is directionally good: train/CV-only calibration, holdout scored once after selection lock, many metrics, and group-block bootstrap. 

Main gaps:

**Primary KPI must be reported as the primary number.** Table III explains why LCB95 Recall@1%FPR is the headline KPI, but the paper does not place that value front and center. 

**Clarify the calibration transform.** “Geodesic-odds” is not a common term in radar or mainstream classifier calibration. It may be correct internally, but reviewers may view it as unnecessary jargon. Consider calling it “monotone log-odds calibration” unless “geodesic” has a formal definition you include.

**Calibration metrics need bin counts.** With rare positives, ECE can look excellent because negatives dominate. Add reliability diagrams with adaptive bins, bin counts, calibration slope/intercept, and Brier decomposition if possible.

**Report false alarms in operational units.** A record-level FPR is useful, but radar people will ask for false alarms per unit time, per scan, per CPI, or per scenario group. Add a proxy FA/hour or FA/group metric if the phase windows are time-defined.

## Section V: Locked-Candidate Transparency and Ablation Results

The idea is very good. You explicitly report the advanced candidate as a locked artifact rather than a fresh rerun, and the evidence bundle includes component aliases, ablations, top-k ablations, modality ablations, and no-calibration ablations. 

The problem is presentation. “evolved_mesh_39/sparse_signed/geodesic_odds” and similar labels are audit handles, not reader-facing scientific names. Keep them in an appendix, but in the main paper translate them into clean component families.

Also, ablation should report deltas, not just values. Use columns:

| Ablation | AP | ΔAP | Recall@≤1%FPR | ΔRecall | ECE | Interpretation |
| -------- | -: | --: | ------------: | ------: | --: | -------------- |

That will make the ablation path interpretable.

## Section VI: Raw Samples and Compare-Only Anchor

This section is important but visually weak. Fig. 7 is useful because it shows positive and hard-negative range-Doppler proxy diagnostics across the three phase windows. Fig. 8, however, is not a convincing measured-anchor comparison. 

The KTH comparison should show distributions, not one compressed bar plot. Use overlaid histograms, violin plots, or empirical CDFs for normalized micro-Doppler bandwidth, range extent, entropy, and return-power proxies. Because KTH is 77 GHz, Doppler should be converted to velocity or normalized before comparison.

## Section VII: Compact Diagnostics Snapshot

This section contains good material: leakage tests, label-shuffle collapse, metadata-only baselines, phase metrics, and false-alarm family table. 

But the “canary forbidden-feature test fail” wording is dangerous. A reviewer may read “fail” and stop. The explanation says the failure is intentional because raw detector views still contain metadata columns that are forbidden at the model interface. That is too confusing.

Rewrite as:

| Check             | Raw audit surface | Model-facing matrix | Interpretation                                                             |
| ----------------- | ----------------: | ------------------: | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Forbidden headers |         Triggered |       Absent / pass | Denylist detects forbidden fields and sanitized model matrix excludes them |

Use “triggered” or “detected,” not “fail.”

Also, do not say the false alarms “concentrate” in single_bird without emphasizing that there is only one false alarm. Say:

> In this holdout, the only observed locked-candidate false alarm is from the single-bird family; this is a useful diagnostic pointer, but larger holdouts are needed before treating it as a stable family-level pattern.

## Section VIII: Evidence Ledger

The evidence-ledger concept is good. Reviewers like artifact traceability. The paper says the evidence bundle includes model cards, feedback coverage, source LOC self-classification audit, split structure, calibration, false-alarm families, ablations, and compare-only anchor checks. 

But this section is too process-heavy for the main paper. The source LOC self-classification and feedback coverage matrix are less important than the radar benchmark itself. Move those to appendix or repository documentation. Keep the main paper focused on:

* artifact list;
* DOI or repository path;
* deterministic seed;
* split manifest;
* detector-view files;
* evaluation script;
* metric traces;
* figure-generation scripts;
* hash/version identifiers.

## Section IX: Limitations and Prohibited Inferences

This section is good but should be more concrete. It correctly states that a synthetic benchmark is not measured truth and that measured anchors are compare-only checks. 

Add explicit limitations:

* only 50 positive groups total;
* only 8 positive groups in default holdout;
* single-seed main corpus;
* simplified clutter/weather/RFI priors;
* 77 GHz anchor mismatch relative to X/Ku/S/GBAD branches;
* limited acoustic/passive-RF physical modeling detail;
* no hardware-in-the-loop or field trial validation;
* low CPI pulse count may limit micro-Doppler fidelity unless long-window aggregation is clearly explained;
* false-alarm-family conclusions are weak with only one observed false alarm.

## Conclusion

The conclusion is safe and clear. It restates that EchoForge is synthetic public-proxy evidence and not operational performance. 

Add one sentence with the main numerical result and one sentence with the next concrete step:

> The locked candidate improves AP and low-FPR recall over the prior layered-fusion baseline on the blind holdout, but the small positive holdout makes group-level uncertainty central to interpretation. The next release should expand positive groups, add multi-seed evaluation, and strengthen measured-anchor normalization.

# Standard visual style to apply across the paper

Use a strict IEEE-style visual system:

**Figures**

* Use vector PDF/SVG wherever possible.
* Use one-column figures only for simple plots; use two-column `figure*` for dense diagnostics.
* Minimum final font size: 8 pt after scaling.
* Avoid more than 4 subpanels in a one-column figure.
* Use readable labels, not audit aliases.
* Use consistent colors across the whole paper:

  * locked candidate / EI: one accent color;
  * baseline: neutral gray;
  * positive class: muted teal or blue;
  * hard negatives: muted categorical palette;
  * warning/audit: amber;
  * prohibited/fail: muted red only when truly bad.
* Use colorblind-safe palettes.
* Add direct annotations sparingly.

**Tables**

* Use `booktabs`, no vertical rules.
* Use `siunitx` decimal alignment.
* Use 2–3 significant digits for metrics.
* Use percentages for FPR/recall where reader intuition matters.
* Bold best values.
* Use light cell shading only for comparison:

  * `green!10` for best/improved;
  * `yellow!12` for caution/mixed;
  * `gray!10` for negligible or not applicable;
  * `red!8` only for true unresolved failures.
* Do not color entire rows unless the status category is the point of the table.
* Sort tables by importance or value, not arbitrary index.
