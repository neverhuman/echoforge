Absolutely — here is the deep technical dump, focused on **defensive detection science** and public-source information. I’m assuming you mean the **Iranian Shahed-136 / Geran-2 family** and, secondarily, the smaller Shahed-131 and newer Shahed-238 variants. Exact radar signatures, mission altitudes, detection ranges, and software performance are not publicly standardized and can be classified or variant-specific, so the best answer is a mix of public platform data, radar physics, and what has been reported from Ukraine and academic counter-UAS work.

## 1. The drone: what “Iranian drone” usually means here

The Shahed-136 is best thought of as a **low-cost, long-range one-way attack UAV**: aerodynamically a small delta-wing aircraft, operationally closer to a slow cruise missile than a reusable drone. RUSI describes the Shahed-136 as launched from a tilting truck-mounted container holding multiple UAVs, using a short-burn rocket assist at launch before flying on its piston engine. It is commonly used for point-to-point strikes against pre-designated targets rather than traditional real-time piloted loitering. ([RUSI][1])

Typical public estimates for the Shahed-136 / Geran-2 family are roughly: **3.5 m length**, **2.5 m wingspan**, **pusher piston engine**, **GNSS/INS guidance**, and a warhead often reported in the **30–50 kg** class, with some later Russian-modified variants reportedly using larger warheads. OSMP describes the base Shahed-131/136 as generally **not remotely piloted** in the classic sense; it flies to preprogrammed GNSS/INS coordinates, although later variants and Russian adaptations complicate that simple picture. ([Open Source Munitions Portal][2])

A useful public speed number for the standard Shahed-136 is about **180–185 km/h**, or roughly **50–51 m/s**. That is extremely slow compared with a cruise missile or jet aircraft, but fast enough that a late first detection can leave only minutes. The jet-powered Shahed-238 is a different case: OSMP reports it around **520 km/h**, so its warning windows are much shorter for the same detection range. ([Open Source Munitions Portal][2])

Range estimates vary. RUSI gives **700–1,000 km** for Shahed-131 and estimates roughly **1,350–1,500 km** for Shahed-136, while other public sources often cite wider bands, sometimes up to or beyond 2,000 km. Treat all range figures as approximate because fuel load, warhead, winds, routing, and variant changes matter. ([RUSI][1])

## 2. Launch and early flight

The Shahed-136 is commonly launched from a **rack or container launcher** using a **rocket-assisted takeoff**. The booster provides the initial acceleration, then the drone transitions to its piston-engine pusher-prop cruise. Public sources do not give a reliable, universal “seconds until stable cruise attitude” number, but the useful engineering distinction is this: the rocket launch phase is short and local; the long detection problem is the low-altitude piston-engine cruise phase. ([RUSI][1])

Radar does **not** need the drone to be in a perfect “final attitude” before detecting it. A radar can detect a target as soon as the target is within line of sight, has enough radar cross section, and is not buried in clutter/noise. But a stable cruise attitude helps with **track confirmation** and **classification**, because the radar can collect repeated range-Doppler observations and, in some systems, propeller micro-Doppler features.

For a defender far from the launch site, detection “at takeoff” is usually unrealistic unless sensors are near the launch area or airborne/spaceborne sensors happen to observe the launch. For ground-based defense, the practical first-detection moment is usually when the drone climbs into the radar’s low-elevation line of sight and produces enough signal above ground clutter.

## 3. The central radar problem: it is low, slow, small, and clutter-adjacent

The Shahed-type target is hard not because it is magically invisible, but because it combines several annoying traits:

| Trait                                            | Why it matters to radar                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| ------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Low altitude**                                 | Earth curvature and terrain block line of sight. The target appears close to the ground return.                                                                                                                 |
| **Small physical size**                          | Lower radar cross section than crewed aircraft.                                                                                                                                                                 |
| **Composite / fiberglass / honeycomb structure** | Can reduce radar return compared with metal aircraft. OSMP notes fiberglass/carbon-fiber-like structures and honeycomb construction intended to reduce radar cross section. ([Open Source Munitions Portal][2]) |
| **Slow speed**                                   | Doppler can overlap with birds, vehicles, wind-blown clutter, or slow airborne objects.                                                                                                                         |
| **Pusher propeller**                             | Creates useful micro-Doppler/acoustic signatures, but these may require close enough range and sufficient dwell time.                                                                                           |
| **Low cost / mass use**                          | Decoys, simultaneous tracks, and saturation stress the sensor-fusion and decision system.                                                                                                                       |

RUSI’s blunt summary is that Shahed-type UAVs are difficult because they are low, slow, low-signature targets; their low altitude can create short detection and engagement windows, and their piston engines create the recognizable “lawnmower” acoustic signature. ([RUSI][1])

Academic counter-UAS radar surveys describe the same core issue more generally: small UAV detection is difficult because of small radar cross section, non-metallic materials, low-altitude flight close to terrain, similarity to birds, and heavy clutter. ([arXiv][3])

## 4. Radar horizon: when can a ground radar even see it?

For low-altitude objects, the first hard limit is often **geometry**, not radar power. A common radar-horizon approximation under standard atmospheric refraction is:

[
R_{km} \approx 4.12 \left(\sqrt{h_r} + \sqrt{h_t}\right)
]

where:

* (R_{km}) is approximate line-of-sight range in kilometers,
* (h_r) is radar antenna height in meters,
* (h_t) is target altitude in meters.

This uses the common “4/3 Earth radius” approximation for standard radar propagation. Real propagation can be worse or better because of terrain, buildings, ducts, refraction, weather, and siting. ([RF Cafe][4])

### Illustrative line-of-sight ranges

Assume flat terrain, no buildings, no hills, no forest, no sea clutter, and adequate radar sensitivity. These are **geometric visibility** numbers, not guaranteed detection numbers.

| Radar antenna height | Drone altitude | Approx. radar horizon | Time at 185 km/h |
| -------------------: | -------------: | --------------------: | ---------------: |
|                 10 m |           10 m |                 26 km |          8.4 min |
|                 10 m |           50 m |                 42 km |         13.6 min |
|                 10 m |          100 m |                 54 km |         17.5 min |
|                 10 m |          300 m |                 84 km |         27.4 min |
|                 30 m |           10 m |                 36 km |         11.5 min |
|                 30 m |           50 m |                 51 km |         16.7 min |
|                 30 m |          100 m |                 63 km |         20.5 min |
|                 30 m |          300 m |                 92 km |         30.0 min |
|                100 m |           10 m |                 54 km |         17.5 min |
|                100 m |           50 m |                 70 km |         22.8 min |
|                100 m |          100 m |                 82 km |         26.7 min |
|                100 m |          300 m |                113 km |         36.8 min |

That table explains why low-elevation detection is so sensitive to both **drone altitude** and **radar antenna height**. A drone at 10–50 m above ground may not be visible to a modest-height ground radar until it is tens of kilometers away, even before considering clutter. A taller radar mast, hilltop radar, elevated building, aerostat, or airborne radar changes the geometry dramatically.

For the jet Shahed-238 at about 520 km/h, the same detection ranges give much shorter warning times: 50 km is only about **5.8 minutes**, 100 km about **11.5 minutes**, and 150 km about **17.3 minutes**. ([Open Source Munitions Portal][2])

## 5. “How high does it need to get to be detected?”

There is no single altitude. It depends on radar height, terrain, clutter, radar sensitivity, waveform, frequency, target aspect, weather, and processing. But for **line-of-sight only**, we can invert the horizon equation.

For a radar antenna at **30 m** height:

| Desired line-of-sight range | Approx. required drone altitude |
| --------------------------: | ------------------------------: |
|                       50 km |                           ~44 m |
|                      100 km |                          ~353 m |
|                      150 km |                          ~957 m |

For a radar antenna at **10 m** height:

| Desired line-of-sight range | Approx. required drone altitude |
| --------------------------: | ------------------------------: |
|                       50 km |                           ~81 m |
|                      100 km |                          ~446 m |
|                      150 km |                        ~1,105 m |

For a radar antenna at **100 m** height:

| Desired line-of-sight range | Approx. required drone altitude |
| --------------------------: | ------------------------------: |
|                       50 km |                            ~5 m |
|                      100 km |                          ~204 m |
|                      150 km |                          ~698 m |

Again: these are **minimum geometric visibility** estimates. A target can be above the radar horizon and still not be detected if its return is too weak or buried in clutter. Conversely, airborne sensors, favorable propagation, or sensor networks may detect low targets earlier than a single ground radar. ([RF Cafe][4])

## 6. Why low elevation is brutal

Low-elevation radar is hard because the radar is looking almost along the ground. That creates several problems at once.

First, there is **ground clutter**. The radar beam illuminates terrain, buildings, trees, vehicles, utility structures, hillsides, waves, and other reflectors. In some cases the clutter return is orders of magnitude stronger than the drone return. A UAV survey notes that clutter from buildings, birds, trees, hills, sea reflections, and terrain can dominate the return, and that clutter cross section can be thousands of times larger than the target return. ([arXiv][3])

Second, there is **terrain masking**. A radar horizon calculation assumes smooth Earth. Real terrain creates shadow zones. A Shahed flying behind ridges, in low valleys, near urban edges, or over uneven terrain can be geometrically hidden even if the simple horizon equation says it should be visible.

Third, there is **multipath**. At low grazing angles, the radar may receive both direct and reflected paths from the ground or sea. These can interfere constructively or destructively, creating fading, ghosting, height errors, or unstable tracks.

Fourth, the target can be **endo-clutter**, meaning its range-Doppler cell is contaminated by clutter rather than isolated cleanly above it. A low-grazing-angle millimeter-wave radar paper specifically frames low-altitude UAV detection as challenging because terrain clutter pollutes the backscatter, requiring adaptive thresholding and time-frequency/micro-Doppler approaches. ([arXiv][5])

Fifth, low elevation causes **beam and resolution problems**. If the radar has a broad elevation beam, the same beam cell can include ground, buildings, and the UAV. Narrower beams and better elevation resolution help, but they cost money, aperture, processing, and scan-management complexity.

## 7. Radar cross section: what do we know?

There does not appear to be a reliable public, measured, authoritative radar cross-section value for the Shahed-136 across frequency, aspect, and configuration. Any single number should be treated skeptically.

Radar cross section depends on:

* radar frequency,
* polarization,
* viewing angle,
* airframe material,
* edge geometry,
* engine and propeller exposure,
* paint/coating,
* payload/warhead configuration,
* whether the radar sees nose, side, underside, or rear aspect.

Academic measurements of a **2 m-class small fixed-wing UAV** found mean RCS values around **−17.6 dBsm** in one X-band scenario and **−22.8 dBsm** over a broader 3–16 GHz modeling set. That corresponds very roughly to about **0.017 m²** and **0.005 m²**, respectively. This is not a Shahed measurement, but it gives a sense of how small fixed-wing UAVs can look to radar. ([MDPI][6])

The Shahed-136 is larger than that 2 m-class example, but its composite/fiberglass/honeycomb structure and delta-wing shape can reduce some returns. OSMP specifically notes the Shahed-136’s fiberglass/carbon-fiber-like construction and honeycomb structure aimed at reducing RCS. ([Open Source Munitions Portal][2])

A key radar-equation point: detection range scales roughly with the **fourth root** of radar cross section. So if the target RCS is 10 times smaller, detection range does not fall by 10 times; it falls by about **1.78×**, meaning the new range is about **56%** of the old range. That fourth-root law is why small changes in radar siting, clutter, and processing can matter as much as raw transmitter power.

## 9. Major radar signal and noise concerns

Here is the practical signal-processing stack of problems.

### Thermal noise

This is the irreducible receiver noise floor. It sets the minimum detectable signal before processing gain, antenna gain, bandwidth, integration time, and thresholds are considered. For small RCS targets, thermal noise matters, but in low-altitude Shahed detection, **clutter is often worse than thermal noise**.

### Ground clutter

This is the dominant problem for low-flying UAVs. Dirt, rocks, buildings, trees, roads, and infrastructure all reflect radar. Low-angle radar cells can contain a massive clutter return plus a small moving drone return. Surveys of UAV radar detection emphasize clutter and false alarms as central limitations for small UAV detection. ([arXiv][3])

### Sea clutter

If the drone is over water or coastal terrain, sea clutter becomes a major issue. Waves produce dynamic returns with Doppler spread, which can mask slow low-flying targets. Sea state, grazing angle, frequency, and polarization all matter.

### Weather and precipitation

Rain, snow, hail, and clouds can add attenuation and clutter. At higher radar frequencies, atmospheric absorption and precipitation effects become more important. Millimeter-wave radars can offer high resolution but often pay a weather/range penalty.

### Birds

Birds are a major false-alarm class. Their size, speed, altitude, and RCS can overlap small UAVs. The difference is often in **micro-Doppler**, track behavior, wingbeat pattern, trajectory, and multi-sensor cues. Academic work reports strong drone-vs-bird classification in controlled datasets, but operational conditions are messier. ([Penn State][7])

### Foliage and wind-blown clutter

Trees, leaves, wind-blown debris, and moving vegetation create dynamic clutter. In rural low-altitude corridors this can be a serious false-alarm source.

### Vehicles and infrastructure

Cars, trains, ships, construction equipment, rotating machinery, and wind turbines can produce Doppler and micro-Doppler signatures. Wind turbines are especially annoying because they create strong rotating-blade returns.

### Multipath

The direct radar echo and a ground-reflected echo can interfere. This can produce fading, angle errors, ghost detections, and unstable altitude estimates.

### Radio-frequency interference

Other radars, communications systems, jammers, electronic warfare activity, poor filtering, and spectrum congestion can reduce detection performance. UAV radar surveys explicitly identify noise, RF interference, clutter, and electronic countermeasures as important limitations. ([arXiv][3])

### Target scintillation

As the drone turns or rocks, its radar cross section fluctuates. The return may brighten or fade depending on aspect angle. A nose-on Shahed may look different from a side-aspect Shahed; the engine, propeller, wing leading edges, and underside all contribute differently.

### Low radial velocity

Doppler radars measure radial velocity, not total speed. A drone moving across the radar’s field of view can have a small radial velocity even if its true ground speed is 185 km/h. That can make it overlap with clutter-rejection filters or slow-moving biological targets.

## 10. What radar methods work best?

There is no single magic detector. The best performance comes from **layered sensing and fusion**.

### Conventional surveillance radar

A 2D or 3D surveillance radar can detect a Shahed-type UAV if it has line of sight, sufficient sensitivity, adequate clutter rejection, and enough update rate. The challenge is that legacy air-defense radars were often optimized for aircraft and missiles, not small, slow, low-altitude composite drones.

### Pulse-Doppler processing

Pulse-Doppler radar separates targets by range and radial velocity. It is very useful against moving aerial targets, but slow low-altitude drones can overlap with clutter, birds, or ground vehicles. The pusher prop can help by adding micro-Doppler structure, but this requires enough signal quality and processing.

### FMCW and millimeter-wave radar

FMCW radars, including Ku/K/mmWave systems, are common in short- to medium-range counter-UAS systems. They can provide high range resolution and useful micro-Doppler information. The tradeoff is that high-frequency systems may be more range- and weather-limited than lower-frequency long-range surveillance radars. A low-grazing-angle millimeter-wave study found that terrain clutter required adaptive thresholding and micro-Doppler/time-frequency processing. ([arXiv][5])

### CFAR detection

CFAR — constant false alarm rate — dynamically adjusts thresholds based on local noise/clutter estimates. It is essential in cluttered UAV detection, but it has a painful tradeoff: set the threshold too low and false alarms explode; set it too high and weak UAVs are missed. Academic UAV radar surveys explicitly emphasize adaptive thresholds and CFAR for small low-flying UAV detection. ([arXiv][3])

### Track-before-detect

For very weak targets, instead of declaring detection from one strong return, the system integrates weak evidence across time and possible tracks. This can help with small RCS targets, but it requires more computation and can be confused by dense clutter or many targets.

### Micro-Doppler classification

Micro-Doppler looks at small periodic motions: rotors, propellers, wingbeats, engine vibration, or moving parts. For quadcopters, rotor micro-Doppler is very distinctive. For Shahed-like fixed-wing drones, the propeller and engine can still be useful, but the signature is not identical to a multirotor. Academic work shows that micro-Doppler spectrograms and machine learning can classify drones and distinguish them from birds with high accuracy in controlled settings. ([Penn State][7])

### Multistatic and passive radar

Passive radar uses illuminators of opportunity — broadcast, cellular, or other signals — and listens for reflections. It can be attractive because it does not transmit, but geometry, synchronization, coverage, and clutter are hard. Multistatic radar can help because a low-RCS shape from one angle may be more visible from another.

### Acoustic detection

The Shahed-136’s piston engine is famously loud. Acoustic networks have become important in Ukraine because they are cheap, passive, and useful against low-altitude drones that radar may see late or inconsistently. U.S. Army reporting describes Ukrainian acoustic systems such as Sky Fortress/Zvook/FENEK using many passive sensors, edge machine learning, and triangulation to cue air defense. ([U.S. Army][8])

Acoustic detection is not a radar replacement. It is range-limited and affected by wind, traffic, industrial noise, sea surf, terrain, temperature gradients, atmospheric absorption, and quiet propulsion. But against a loud piston-engine Shahed-type target, acoustic networks can be very valuable as a low-cost cueing layer. 

### EO/IR

Electro-optical and infrared sensors are useful for confirmation and terminal tracking, especially once cued by radar or acoustic sensors. They are degraded by weather, smoke, darkness for EO, background clutter, and line of sight. IR may see engine/thermal features, but small low-cost drones are still challenging.

## 11. Accuracy: what “best detection methods” can actually claim

This is where precision matters. “Accuracy” can mean at least five different things:

1. **Detection probability**: did the system detect something?
2. **False alarm rate**: how often does it hallucinate targets?
3. **Classification accuracy**: drone vs bird vs aircraft vs clutter?
4. **Track accuracy**: how good are range, azimuth, elevation, and velocity estimates?
5. **Operational kill/intercept rate**: was the target defeated?

These are not interchangeable. A system can detect a drone but misclassify it, classify it correctly but too late, or detect it accurately but fail to intercept it.

In academic settings, radar micro-Doppler classifiers can score very high. One Penn State micro-Doppler study using a custom 10 GHz radar reported around **96% drone-vs-bird classification**, around **90% drone size classification**, and about **85% accuracy** for distinguishing among five individual classes. Those are controlled experimental results, not guaranteed wartime Shahed detection performance. ([Penn State][7])

Another K-band FMCW radar study using micro-Doppler and range-period features reported classification performance as high as **99.2%** in a short-range setting and **97.5%** in a long-range setting for its tested drones/bird dataset. The same paper reports detection probability rising from about **75.9% at −6 dB SNR** to **99.1% at 5 dB SNR** under its experiment assumptions, with a stated false-alarm setting. Again, this is a specific experimental radar/dataset context, not a universal Shahed number. ([MDPI][9])

Operationally, reported shootdown/interception percentages in Ukraine are **not pure radar detection accuracy**. RUSI cited historical Ukrainian claims such as **50–70%** defense success rates and a later claim of **85%** of a batch shot down, but those reflect a full defense ecosystem: radar, acoustic cueing, visual observers, mobile fire groups, EW, missiles, guns, weather, target routing, and saturation conditions. ([RUSI][1])

A U.S. Army report on acoustic drone detection describes Ukrainian acoustic networks cueing local defenses and mentions reported mass sensor deployments and successful intercept events, but those results should be read as **sensor-fusion and air-defense outcomes**, not as standalone acoustic or radar accuracy. 

The most honest answer is: **best-in-class controlled classifiers can exceed 95% in narrow test conditions; real-world Shahed detection performance is scenario-dependent and often dominated by radar horizon, clutter, sensor density, fusion quality, saturation, and rules for false alarms.**

## 12. Sampling window: how much data is needed?

A radar can produce a raw detection from a very short pulse/chirp sequence, but a reliable defensive alert usually needs more than a single return.

Think of it in layers:

| Layer                              |                        Typical time scale | Purpose                                   |
| ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------: | ----------------------------------------- |
| Pulse/chirp return                 |              microseconds to milliseconds | Raw range/velocity measurement            |
| Coherent processing interval       |          tens to hundreds of milliseconds | Doppler estimate, SNR gain                |
| Micro-Doppler/classification frame | hundreds of milliseconds to a few seconds | Propeller/rotor/wingbeat features         |
| Track confirmation                 |           multiple updates, often seconds | Confirm persistent moving target          |
| Sensor-fusion confidence           |                seconds to tens of seconds | Correlate radar/acoustic/EO/other reports |

One public K-band FMCW drone/bird radar study used radar modulation periods from **0.2–40 ms** and constructed samples of about **0.4 seconds** for classification. That is a useful academic anchor: sub-second windows can support classification in controlled data, but operational confidence often requires several sequential detections and track updates. ([MDPI][9])

For a Shahed-type fixed-wing drone, classification improves when the drone is in stable cruise because the propeller/engine signature, speed, altitude, and track behavior become consistent. But detection itself does not require “final attitude.” The system may detect an unstable or maneuvering target; it just may classify and track it with less confidence until the returns stabilize.

## 13. How long does it need to be flying at final attitude?

For **basic radar detection**, essentially no special “final attitude” dwell is required beyond line of sight and sufficient return. A radar could detect it during climb-out, transition, cruise, or terminal phase if the geometry and SNR are good.

For **classification**, you generally want a persistent track and enough temporal data to observe characteristic features. In academic micro-Doppler work, useful windows can be sub-second to several seconds; in real defense systems, the alert logic often wants multiple hits over time to avoid false alarms from birds, vehicles, clutter spikes, or multipath. A reasonable public-science answer is:

* **Detection:** possible as soon as the return clears horizon/clutter thresholds.
* **Track initiation:** often needs multiple detections over seconds, depending on radar update rate.
* **Classification:** can use sub-second windows in controlled radar datasets, but robust field classification usually benefits from several seconds plus sensor fusion.
* **Engagement-quality track:** depends heavily on the weapon/sensor system and is not reducible to one public number.

## 14. Low/high altitude: what is typical?

Public sources consistently describe Shahed-type attacks as **low-altitude** or “low and slow,” but there is no reliable public fixed cruise altitude that applies across all variants, missions, routes, and Russian/Iranian adaptations. RUSI emphasizes low-altitude, nap-of-the-earth-style profiles and the resulting short detection window. ([RUSI][1])

From a radar-planning perspective, the useful altitude bands are:

| Altitude band                  | Detection implication                                                                                          |
| ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Very low: ~10–50 m AGL**     | Severe horizon and clutter problem. Detection may be delayed to tens of km or less for ordinary ground radars. |
| **Low: ~50–150 m AGL**         | Still clutter/horizon limited, but visible farther to elevated radars.                                         |
| **Medium low: ~150–500 m AGL** | Better radar horizon; still small-RCS/clutter problem.                                                         |
| **Higher transit: >500 m AGL** | More visible to radar, but still may be difficult if RCS is low or saturation/decoys are present.              |

Those are analytic bands, not a claim that every Shahed flies a specific altitude. The important thing is that even a shift from 30 m to 300 m can radically change first-detection range.

## 15. The best defensive detection architecture

The strongest public answer is **sensor fusion**, not one radar.

A good defensive architecture against Shahed-type drones would combine:

1. **Elevated low-altitude radar coverage**
   Put sensors where terrain/horizon problems are reduced. Elevated radars see low targets sooner.

2. **Short- and medium-range counter-UAS radars**
   Use high update rate and good range-Doppler processing for local defense.

3. **Acoustic networks**
   Especially effective against loud piston-engine Shahed-type drones; cheap sensors can fill gaps and cue other systems. U.S. Army reporting describes Ukraine’s acoustic systems using many passive nodes, ML classification, triangulation, and fusion with radar/EO/IR. ([U.S. Army][8])

4. **EO/IR confirmation**
   Useful once cued, especially in terminal defense.

5. **Passive/multistatic sensing**
   Can provide additional geometries and reduce dependence on one active radar.

6. **Data fusion and track management**
   The hard part in mass attacks is not merely seeing one target; it is correlating many weak tracks, rejecting false alarms, prioritizing threats, and maintaining confidence under saturation.

7. **Adaptive clutter maps and CFAR-like processing**
   These help keep false alarms manageable while preserving weak-target sensitivity. UAV radar literature repeatedly points to adaptive thresholding, clutter rejection, and micro-Doppler as key tools. ([arXiv][3])

## 16. Why it may be detected acoustically before radar in some cases

This sounds counterintuitive, but for a loud piston-engine UAV flying very low, a distributed acoustic sensor near the path may hear it before a ground radar has a clean line of sight or clean clutter separation. The Shahed’s “lawnmower” acoustic signature is widely noted, and the U.S. Army report describes acoustic networks as a practical response to low-RCS, low-altitude, slow drones that complicate legacy radar. ([RUSI][1])

The tradeoff is range. Acoustic sensors are usually local warning and cueing sensors, not long-range air-surveillance systems. The U.S. Army report notes limitations such as urban/industrial noise, sea surf, wind, temperature gradients, atmospheric absorption, and quieter propulsion, with practical ranges often in the hundreds to a few thousand meters depending on conditions. 

## 17. The most important “gotchas”

The biggest mistake is asking, “At what range can radar detect a Shahed?” as though there is one answer. The better question is:

> At what range can **this radar**, at **this antenna height**, in **this terrain**, at **this frequency**, against **this aspect angle**, with **this clutter**, using **this threshold**, detect and track **this variant** with acceptable false-alarm probability?

The second biggest mistake is confusing **detection** with **classification**. A radar may detect a weak object but not know whether it is a Shahed, bird flock, small aircraft, decoy, or clutter artifact. Classification requires additional features, time, sensor fusion, or human/automated confirmation.

The third biggest mistake is assuming low speed makes the Shahed easy. Low speed gives defenders time **only if detection is early**. Low speed also makes the target more likely to overlap with birds, vehicles, and ground clutter in Doppler space.

The fourth biggest mistake is ignoring saturation. A single Shahed is a radar-detection problem. Dozens or hundreds of drones, decoys, and missiles are a **system-capacity problem**. Recent reporting on Russia’s use of Shahed-type UAVs describes very large launch volumes and mixed inventories including strike UAVs and decoys, which changes the detection problem into a track-management and prioritization problem. ([ISIS][10])

For acoustic detection and Ukraine-style sensor networks, the U.S. Army “Listening to the Sky” report is directly relevant. It explains why passive acoustic networks are useful against low-altitude drones and how they can cue radar, EO/IR, and mobile defenses. ([U.S. Army][8])

## Bottom line

A standard Shahed-136 is roughly a **slow, low-altitude, small-RCS, piston-engine, GNSS-guided one-way attack aircraft**. Its speed gives defenders time only if it is detected early. The hard part is that low altitude creates **radar horizon limits**, **terrain masking**, **ground clutter**, **multipath**, and **false-alarm confusion** with birds and other slow objects.

For a typical ground radar, first clean detection might be limited to **tens of kilometers** if the drone is very low, giving only **minutes** of warning. With elevated radars, good terrain, sensor fusion, acoustic cueing, and robust processing, detection can move much earlier. The best systems are not just “better radars”; they are **networks**: radar plus acoustic plus EO/IR plus passive sensing plus strong track fusion.
