I’ll treat **“the Iranian drone”** as the **Shahed-136 / Geran-2 class one-way attack UAV**, while noting that Iran also has other UAVs such as Shahed-131, Mohajer-6, Shahed-238/jet variants, etc. I’m keeping this to **public-domain defensive radar science**: enough to understand detection, warning time, clutter, accuracy, and sensor design, but not real-time launch patterns or sensor-specific exploitation details.

## 1. What the Shahed-136/Geran-2 is

The Shahed-136 is best thought of less as a “drone” in the hobbyist sense and more as a **slow, low-cost, pre-programmed flying munition**. Open-source descriptions characterize it as a one-way attack UAV that flies toward pre-programmed coordinates using GNSS/INS-style navigation rather than being remotely piloted in the usual sense. Public estimates commonly put it around **3.5 m long**, **2.5 m wingspan**, with a **tens-of-kilograms warhead**, and a **range somewhere in the 1,000–2,000+ km class**, depending on variant and source. ([Open Source Munitions Portal][1])

A useful public technical profile from RUSI describes the Shahed-136 as a scaled-up Shahed-131, using a simple composite/honeycomb airframe, commercial-grade avionics, and a rear piston engine. RUSI also describes the launch method: multiple UAVs can be carried on a truck-mounted launcher, with each aircraft catapulted/boosted into the air by a short-burn rocket motor before normal engine-powered flight. ([RUSI][2])

| Feature         | Public open-source picture                                                                        |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Role            | One-way attack UAV / loitering munition / “pseudo-missile”                                        |
| Guidance        | Pre-programmed route/target; GNSS/INS-type navigation                                             |
| Size            | Roughly 3.5 m length, 2.5 m wingspan for Shahed-136                                               |
| Warhead         | Often cited around 30–50 kg; later variants may differ                                            |
| Propulsion      | Rear pusher piston engine; public sources associate it with MD-550/Limbach-derived engine family  |
| Launch          | Rail/catapult/rocket-assisted launch, often from mobile launcher                                  |
| Speed           | Common public estimate around **185 km/h** for piston Shahed-136; jet derivatives are much faster |
| Altitude        | Open sources give a broad envelope; low-altitude use is central to the detection challenge        |
| Radar character | Small, slow, low-flying, composite-bodied, aspect-dependent RCS                                   |

Army Recognition gives one common public specification set: about **185 km/h max speed**, **1,000–2,500 km range**, **30–50 kg warhead**, and an altitude envelope cited as **60–4,000 m**. Treat those values as open-source estimates rather than guaranteed constants; variants, load, route, weather, and operator choices matter. ([Army Recognition][3])

Jet-powered Shahed/Geran derivatives are a different detection problem. CSIS notes reported jet-powered variants with speeds in the **550–600 km/h** range, which dramatically compresses warning time compared with the piston Shahed-136 class. ([CSIS][4])

## 2. When does it “take off”?

There is no inherent, universal “takeoff time.” It is launched when the operator chooses. The vehicle itself does not need a runway in the normal aircraft sense. Public descriptions indicate a **rocket-assisted rail/catapult launch**, after which the piston engine sustains cruise flight. ([RUSI][2])

From a radar perspective, the launch sequence has a few phases:

1. **On launcher / pre-launch**: a radar usually does not detect it as an airborne target. It might appear as part of a ground vehicle or structure if the radar is looking at the area, but that is a different surveillance problem.

2. **Boost / initial climb**: the object becomes airborne, but the track can be short, accelerating, low, and close to ground clutter. If a radar has direct line of sight to the launch area, detection could in principle occur quickly. If the radar is far away, the target will be hidden by Earth curvature, terrain, buildings, vegetation, or low-elevation clutter until it climbs or approaches.

3. **Steady cruise**: this is usually the most radar-trackable phase: more stable speed, course, and altitude; consistent Doppler; longer observation time.

4. **Terminal phase**: route-dependent. It may descend, maneuver, or dive depending on mission programming and variant. Public sources do not support a single universal “final attitude time” for all Shahed attacks.

So the meaningful question is not “how many seconds after takeoff?” but:

> At what range and altitude does the drone rise above the radar horizon, and does it produce enough signal-to-clutter ratio to be declared a real track?

That depends heavily on radar height, drone altitude, terrain, frequency band, clutter, radar processing, weather, and aspect angle.

## 3. Why low elevation is such a huge problem

Low altitude hurts detection in several overlapping ways.

First is **geometry**: the Earth is curved. A ground radar cannot see indefinitely along the surface. Atmospheric refraction bends radar beams somewhat, so radar engineers often use an “effective Earth radius” approximation, commonly the **4/3 Earth model**, but that only extends the horizon; it does not remove it. MIT Lincoln Laboratory’s radar propagation material emphasizes that low-altitude detection is affected by Earth curvature, diffraction, refraction, multipath, and atmospheric effects. ([Lincoln Laboratory][5])

Second is **clutter**. At low elevation angles, the radar beam intersects ground, sea, buildings, trees, terrain, vehicles, birds, wind turbines, and weather. Those returns can be much stronger than the small UAV. MIT’s radar clutter material notes that clutter depends on frequency, geometry, terrain type, resolution, multipath, sea state, grazing angle, weather, and many other variables; sea clutter at low grazing angle can produce strong “spikes” that cause false detections. ([Lincoln Laboratory][6])

Third is **multipath**. A low target often produces both a direct radar reflection and a ground-reflected path. Those two paths can interfere constructively or destructively. MIT’s propagation notes describe lobed elevation coverage where a target echo can be weaker than free-space expectations or even fall into a null. ([Lincoln Laboratory][5])

Fourth is **small and variable radar cross section**. The Shahed is much smaller than a crewed aircraft, uses composite/fiberglass-style structures, and has an aspect-dependent shape. OSMP notes the use of fiberglass/carbon-fiber-like composite/honeycomb structures intended to reduce radar detectability, while radar-RCS research on small fixed-wing UAVs shows how strongly small-UAV RCS varies with aspect, frequency, and airframe geometry. ([Open Source Munitions Portal][7])

Fifth is **Doppler ambiguity with clutter and birds**. A Shahed-136 is slow by aircraft standards. A head-on target at 185 km/h has useful Doppler, but a crossing target has much lower radial velocity. Low radial velocity can push the target closer to ground/sea/biological clutter. UAV radar surveys identify small RCS, bird similarity, low-altitude flight, terrain proximity, and clutter as core problems for drone radar detection. ([arXiv][8])

## 4. How high does it need to be to be detected?

There is no single answer, but there is a very useful first-order radar-horizon calculation.

Using the common 4/3 Earth approximation:

[
R_\text{LOS} \approx 4.12 \left(\sqrt{h_r} + \sqrt{h_t}\right)
]

where:

* (R_\text{LOS}) is approximate line-of-sight range in kilometers,
* (h_r) is radar antenna height in meters,
* (h_t) is target altitude in meters.

This is only a **geometric line-of-sight upper bound**. Real detection may occur later because of terrain, clutter, weak RCS, weather, masking, radar thresholds, and track-confirmation requirements. MIT’s propagation notes are a good public foundation for why horizon, refraction, diffraction, and multipath matter. ([Lincoln Laboratory][5])

Assuming a piston Shahed speed of about **185 km/h**, or **51.4 m/s**, the following table gives rough line-of-sight range and maximum possible warning time. These are not guaranteed detection ranges.

|    Radar antenna height |     Drone at 60 m |    Drone at 100 m |    Drone at 300 m |   Drone at 1,000 m |
| ----------------------: | ----------------: | ----------------: | ----------------: | -----------------: |
|                    10 m |  ~45 km / ~15 min |  ~54 km / ~18 min |  ~84 km / ~27 min |  ~143 km / ~47 min |
|                    30 m |  ~55 km / ~18 min |  ~64 km / ~21 min |  ~94 km / ~31 min |  ~153 km / ~50 min |
|                   100 m |  ~73 km / ~24 min |  ~82 km / ~27 min | ~113 km / ~37 min |  ~172 km / ~56 min |
|                   300 m | ~103 km / ~34 min | ~113 km / ~37 min | ~143 km / ~46 min |  ~202 km / ~65 min |
| 3,000 m airborne sensor | ~258 km / ~84 min | ~267 km / ~87 min | ~297 km / ~96 min | ~356 km / ~115 min |

The practical lesson is huge: **elevating the sensor helps a lot**. A ground radar at 30 m antenna height seeing a 60 m target might have only tens of kilometers of geometric horizon. An airborne or high-elevation sensor can extend that dramatically. But again, horizon is not detection; it is only the first gate.

The reverse calculation is also useful: how high would the target need to be to be line-of-sight at a given range?

| Radar antenna height | 50 km range | 100 km range | 150 km range | 200 km range |
| -------------------: | ----------: | -----------: | -----------: | -----------: |
|                 10 m |       ~81 m |       ~446 m |     ~1,105 m |     ~2,060 m |
|                 30 m |       ~44 m |       ~353 m |       ~957 m |     ~1,855 m |
|                100 m |        ~5 m |       ~204 m |       ~697 m |     ~1,486 m |

These numbers are useful for defensive sensor planning, not as “safe fly-under” values. Real integrated air-defense networks may include elevated radars, airborne sensors, passive sensors, acoustic arrays, EO/IR, multistatic geometries, and local observers, all of which can change the detection picture.

## 5. How soon can it be detected after launch?

For a **nearby radar with direct line of sight**, detection could theoretically happen shortly after it becomes airborne and separates from ground clutter. In practice, declaration of a reliable track usually needs more than one return: the system wants enough range, Doppler, angle, and repeated detections to avoid false alarms.

For a **distant ground radar**, the delay is dominated by geometry. A low-altitude Shahed might not be visible until it either:

* climbs above the local radar horizon,
* emerges from terrain/urban masking,
* gets close enough that the radar can see over the curvature/terrain,
* or is picked up by another sensor layer.

At 185 km/h, the time from first possible line-of-sight to arrival is roughly:

| First reliable detection range | Time to arrival at 185 km/h |
| -----------------------------: | --------------------------: |
|                          20 km |                ~6.5 minutes |
|                          50 km |                 ~16 minutes |
|                          75 km |                 ~24 minutes |
|                         100 km |                 ~32 minutes |
|                         150 km |                 ~49 minutes |
|                         200 km |                 ~65 minutes |

For a **550–600 km/h jet variant**, those windows shrink to roughly one-third of the piston-Shahed values. That is why faster variants are much harder for point-defense and mobile teams even if the radar horizon is unchanged. ([CSIS][4])

## 6. How low or high does it typically fly?

Public sources give broad capability envelopes, but actual mission altitude is route- and operator-dependent. Army Recognition gives a public altitude envelope of **60–4,000 m** for Shahed-136, while OSMP and RUSI emphasize the platform’s low-cost, low-altitude, long-range attack role rather than a fixed universal cruise altitude. ([Army Recognition][3])

Operationally, the detection challenge comes from the fact that these systems can spend significant time at **low altitude**, where radar horizon and clutter are most severe. But they do not need to fly at one constant altitude for the whole route. A pre-programmed route can include waypoints, altitude changes, turns, and terminal behavior. Public material does not support a precise universal value such as “it must fly X minutes at final attitude.”

For radar, the important phases are:

| Phase                               | Radar relevance                                                                            |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Launch/boost                        | Short, low, accelerating, cluttered; may be hard to form a track unless nearby and visible |
| Transition to engine-powered flight | Changing speed/aspect; track filters may need confirmation                                 |
| Cruise                              | Best phase for stable radar tracking; steady velocity and course help Doppler/filtering    |
| Terminal                            | Can reduce warning time if lower, masked, or maneuvering; exact behavior varies            |

A reasonable defensive assumption is: **do not rely on terminal-phase detection**. The earlier the sensor stack can detect the drone during cruise, the better.

## 7. Radar cross section: how “stealthy” is it?

The Shahed-136 is not a high-end stealth aircraft, but it is a difficult radar target because it is:

* small compared with aircraft,
* often low,
* relatively slow,
* partly composite,
* aspect-dependent,
* flying in clutter-rich background,
* and potentially used in groups with decoys or other missiles.

Composite materials and honeycomb construction can reduce some radar returns, but the vehicle still has radar-reflective contributors: engine, propeller, warhead, wiring, structural edges, and geometry. OSMP notes composite/honeycomb construction intended to reduce RCS, while fixed-wing small-UAV RCS research shows that even small fixed-wing drones can have highly variable RCS depending on aspect and frequency. ([Open Source Munitions Portal][7])

The radar equation matters here:

[
P_r \propto \frac{\sigma}{R^4}
]

where (P_r) is received power, (R) is range, and (\sigma) is radar cross section. Because of the fourth-power range law, small changes in range matter enormously. Because (\sigma) varies by aspect and material, a drone can be easier to detect from one angle than another.

A key implication: **a 16× reduction in RCS only halves ideal detection range**, but real-world clutter and horizon can dominate more than pure RCS.

## 8. Major radar signal/noise/clutter concerns

The main problems are not just “noise” in the thermal sense. They are **signal-to-clutter**, **false alarms**, and **track confidence**.

### Ground clutter

Low-elevation radar beams hit terrain, buildings, trees, roads, and vehicles. The clutter return can be much stronger than the drone. UAV radar surveys specifically call out buildings, man-made structures, birds, trees, hills, ground/sea returns, moving cars, wind turbines, foliage, and precipitation as interfering returns. ([arXiv][8])

### Sea clutter

Over water, low grazing angles are brutal. MIT’s radar clutter material notes that sea clutter depends on wind, weather, sea state, frequency, polarization, resolution, and grazing angle; low-grazing-angle sea “spikes” can cause excessive false detections. ([Lincoln Laboratory][6])

### Weather clutter

Rain, hail, snow, and fog can superimpose echoes on the target. MIT notes that rain both attenuates and reflects radar energy, and that rain clutter becomes more significant at higher frequencies such as X-band. ([Lincoln Laboratory][6])

### Biological clutter

Birds are a classic problem. Their RCS and speed can overlap with small UAVs. Micro-Doppler helps because birds flap while propeller aircraft have rotating blades, but classification is still aspect-, range-, and data-dependent. Drone/bird discrimination research reports strong results under controlled conditions, but also highlights dataset and viewing-angle limitations. ([MDPI][9])

### Multipath and elevation nulls

At low altitude, the radar receives direct and reflected energy. These can interfere and create coverage lobes or nulls. MIT’s propagation notes describe how multipath can make an echo weaker than free-space prediction or disappear in a null. ([Lincoln Laboratory][5])

### Doppler clutter

A slow or crossing target may have low radial velocity. If its Doppler overlaps with ground vehicles, wind-blown foliage, sea clutter, or birds, detection gets harder.

For a 185 km/h target:

[
v \approx 51.4 \text{ m/s}
]

Monostatic Doppler shift is approximately:

[
f_D = \frac{2v_r}{\lambda}
]

For a head-on target:

| Band   | Approx wavelength | Head-on Doppler at 185 km/h |
| ------ | ----------------: | --------------------------: |
| L-band |           ~0.23 m |                     ~450 Hz |
| S-band |           ~0.10 m |                   ~1,030 Hz |
| X-band |           ~0.03 m |                   ~3,400 Hz |

But if the drone is crossing the radar rather than approaching it, radial velocity (v_r) is lower, and the Doppler advantage shrinks.

### RF interference and jamming

Radar receivers also face ordinary thermal noise, receiver noise figure, phase noise, sidelobe interference, other emitters, and possibly deliberate electronic attack. Publicly, the more general point is that classification confidence drops when the system has low SNR, high clutter, or degraded tracks.

### Saturation and decoys

Modern Shahed attacks are often not single-aircraft events. Public campaign analysis describes mass launches, decoys, mixed packages, and saturation pressure on defenses. That creates a C2 and classification problem: not merely “can I see one drone?” but “can I track, classify, prioritize, and engage many objects at once?” ([ISIS][10])

## 9. Best detection approaches

The best answer is not one magic radar. It is a **layered sensor network**.

### 1. Elevated or airborne radar

This is the biggest geometric improvement. Raising the radar dramatically extends low-altitude line of sight. Airborne early warning, aerostat-mounted sensors, hilltop radars, towers, and elevated masts all help defeat the horizon problem.

### 2. 3D pulse-Doppler / AESA surveillance radar

Modern 3D radar gives range, azimuth, elevation, and Doppler. AESA systems can revisit sectors quickly, adapt dwell time, and form tracks more robustly. Pulse-Doppler processing helps separate moving targets from stationary clutter.

### 3. Short-range counter-UAS radar

High-frequency X/Ku-band radars can provide good resolution and micro-Doppler detail at shorter ranges. They are useful around protected sites, but weather attenuation, horizon, and clutter still matter.

### 4. Multistatic/passive radar

Multiple separated receivers/transmitters can see geometries a monostatic radar might miss. Passive systems can exploit existing illuminators. They are useful complements, especially against low-RCS or low-altitude targets, but they require careful synchronization, geometry, and processing.

### 5. Acoustic arrays

Shahed-type UAVs have distinctive piston/propeller acoustic signatures. The U.S. Army CALL report on Ukraine describes low-cost acoustic monitoring networks using microphones, local machine-learning classification, time synchronization, bearing/confidence reports, triangulation, and fusion with other sensors. It also notes limitations: ambient noise, wind, temperature gradients, sea surf, masking, quiet propulsion, and limited local range. 

Acoustic systems are not a radar replacement. They are excellent as a **cueing layer**: “something Shahed-like is coming from this bearing,” allowing radar, EO/IR, mobile teams, and air-defense systems to focus attention.

### 6. EO/IR cameras

Electro-optical and infrared sensors can classify and confirm tracks, especially after radar or acoustic cueing. They are affected by weather, cloud, haze, darkness, background, and line of sight.

### 7. Data fusion

The strongest systems combine radar, acoustic, EO/IR, passive RF, human reporting, and command-and-control. CALL describes systems that correlate multiple acoustic detections and fuse them with other sensors to cue defenses. 

## 10. How accurate are the best detection methods?

There is no universal public number like “Shahed radar detection is 97.3% accurate.” Accuracy depends on:

* radar type,
* antenna height,
* frequency,
* waveform,
* target altitude,
* target aspect,
* weather,
* terrain,
* clutter,
* number of simultaneous targets,
* false-alarm tolerance,
* classification threshold,
* track-confirmation logic,
* and whether “accuracy” means detection probability, classification accuracy, localization accuracy, track continuity, or successful intercept.

That said, public research gives some useful benchmarks.

A 10 GHz continuous-wave radar study using micro-Doppler spectrograms and machine learning reported about **96% drone-vs-bird discrimination**, about **90% drone size classification**, and about **85% classification across five individual classes**, but the authors also noted important limitations: controlled data, limited viewing angles, and non-natural bird conditions. ([MDPI][9])

That result is encouraging, but it is not a guarantee for Shahed detection in combat. A Shahed is larger than many hobby drones, but it may be farther away, lower, partly composite, in clutter, in groups, at night, in weather, and possibly accompanied by decoys.

For acoustic networks, CALL cites public reporting that Ukraine deployed thousands of acoustic sensors and used them to cue defenses against one-way attack UAVs, including a reported attack in which **80 of 84 UAVs** were intercepted. But that number is an engagement outcome, not a pure sensor classification metric. 

A defensible summary:

| Method                               | Detection strength                   | Main weakness                               | Public accuracy picture                                                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| High-quality elevated/airborne radar | Best for early warning               | Cost, coverage, terrain, saturation         | Can be excellent when target is in line of sight and above clutter; public Shahed-specific Pd/Pfa not generally available |
| Ground surveillance radar            | Good where geometry is favorable     | Horizon, clutter, low elevation             | Highly site-dependent                                                                                                     |
| Short-range counter-UAS radar        | Good local protection/classification | Shorter range, weather/clutter              | Research micro-Doppler can be strong under controlled conditions                                                          |
| Acoustic arrays                      | Cheap, scalable cueing               | Wind, urban/industrial noise, limited range | Strong as cueing/fusion layer; not a standalone universal detector                                                        |
| EO/IR                                | Excellent confirmation               | Weather/visibility/line of sight            | Best after cueing                                                                                                         |
| Fused network                        | Best real-world answer               | Integration complexity                      | Most robust against false alarms, clutter, and saturation                                                                 |

## 11. What sampling window is needed?

This depends on the task: detection, tracking, or classification.

### Detection

A radar can get an initial detection in one dwell/coherent processing interval if SNR and clutter conditions are good. But a single hit is usually not enough to declare a reliable hostile track. Radar systems normally require repeated consistency in range, Doppler, azimuth/elevation, and motion model.

### Tracking

Track initiation generally needs multiple detections over time. For rotating surveillance radars, this can mean several scans; for staring AESA or dedicated counter-UAS radar, it can be faster. A safe public-level statement is:

> Reliable track formation is usually seconds to tens of seconds, depending on scan rate, dwell time, SNR, clutter, and track-confirmation logic.

### Doppler velocity resolution

The coherent processing interval matters. A simplified velocity-resolution relationship is:

[
\Delta v \approx \frac{\lambda}{2T_\text{CPI}}
]

where (T_\text{CPI}) is coherent integration time. Longer coherent processing improves velocity resolution, but there are tradeoffs: target maneuver, scan schedule, clutter stationarity, and computational load.

Example:

|   CPI | L-band velocity resolution, rough | S/X-band qualitative effect                                                        |
| ----: | --------------------------------: | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 0.1 s |                           Coarser | Faster update, poorer Doppler precision                                            |
| 0.5 s |                            Better | Often enough to separate many moving targets from clutter if geometry is favorable |
| 1.0 s |                      Better still | Useful for classification, but dwell/scan tradeoffs matter                         |

These are physics-level statements, not recommended settings for a particular radar.

### Micro-Doppler classification

To classify a propeller-driven UAV, the radar benefits from a short time history of the rotating propeller, body motion, and flight kinematics. Micro-Doppler spectrogram approaches often need **sub-second to several-second** observation windows, depending on frequency, aspect, SNR, and algorithm. The drone-vs-bird radar study used micro-Doppler spectrogram imagery and achieved strong controlled-condition performance, but its limitations show why real-world accuracy needs wider aspect and environmental coverage. ([MDPI][9])

### Acoustic classification

Acoustic systems similarly need enough samples to identify engine/propeller signatures and enough time-synchronized observations from multiple nodes to triangulate. CALL describes acoustic nodes sending timestamped bearing/confidence data and fusing multiple detections to estimate track, speed, and heading. 

A practical sampling summary:

| Goal                               | Typical public-level observation need                                    |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| First radar hit                    | One dwell/CPI, if SNR and clutter allow                                  |
| Reliable radar track               | Multiple consistent detections; seconds to tens of seconds               |
| Radar micro-Doppler classification | Sub-second to several-second spectrograms; longer if weak/aspect-limited |
| Acoustic classification            | Seconds-scale engine/propeller signature plus repeated detections        |
| Multi-sensor confidence            | Radar/acoustic/EO correlation over time; more robust than any single hit |

## 12. What makes Shahed detection different from detecting a normal aircraft?

A normal aircraft is usually:

* larger,
* faster,
* higher,
* more metallic,
* easier to separate from clutter,
* and easier to track at long range.

A Shahed-136-class target is:

* smaller,
* slower,
* lower,
* often composite,
* potentially close to bird speeds/RCS classes,
* acoustically distinctive but radar-challenging,
* cheap enough to be used in large numbers,
* and operationally useful precisely because it stresses the defender’s sensor and interceptor economics.

That combination is nasty. A high-end missile-defense radar may detect it, but using expensive interceptors against cheap drones can be economically painful. That is why Ukraine and others emphasize layered detection: radar for coverage, acoustic for cheap cueing, EO/IR for confirmation, mobile fire teams or low-cost interceptors for engagement, and C2 fusion to avoid wasting shots.

## 13. The biggest scientific issues in one place

### Radar horizon

Low-altitude targets are hidden by curvature and terrain. Raising the radar or target altitude extends line of sight. This is often the first limiting factor. ([Lincoln Laboratory][5])

### Signal-to-clutter ratio

The target may be visible in theory but buried in ground, sea, weather, bird, vehicle, or foliage clutter. ([Lincoln Laboratory][6])

### Aspect-dependent RCS

Small UAV RCS is not one number. It changes with angle, frequency, polarization, and airframe details. ([MDPI][11])

### Doppler ambiguity

Slow or crossing targets can sit near clutter in Doppler space.

### Multipath

Low-altitude direct and reflected paths can create lobes and nulls. ([Lincoln Laboratory][5])

### False alarms

Birds, wind turbines, vehicles, weather, and sea spikes can look target-like. Good detection requires controlling false alarm probability, not just maximizing sensitivity.

### Classification versus detection

Detecting “something moving” is easier than confidently saying “Shahed-136-like one-way attack UAV.” Classification needs more features: kinematics, micro-Doppler, acoustic signature, track behavior, size estimate, EO/IR confirmation, and sometimes intelligence context.

### Saturation

Mass launches change the problem from single-target detection to multi-target tracking, prioritization, and engagement management. Public reporting on recent Shahed/Geran campaigns shows very large launch volumes and mixed packages designed to stress air defenses. ([ISIS][10])

For the **Shahed-136 technical profile**, RUSI’s technical profile is one of the better public summaries of construction, propulsion, launch, avionics, and range estimates. ([RUSI][2])

For **radar propagation, horizon, multipath, diffraction, and refraction**, MIT Lincoln Laboratory’s radar lecture material is a strong public technical foundation. ([Lincoln Laboratory][5])

For **clutter, weather, sea clutter, biological clutter, and false alarms**, MIT’s radar clutter material and UAV radar survey literature are useful. ([Lincoln Laboratory][6])

For **small-UAV RCS**, the fixed-wing UAV radar cross-section literature is valuable because it shows how much aspect angle, frequency, and geometry affect detectability. ([MDPI][11])

For **drone/bird radar micro-Doppler classification**, the 10 GHz radar micro-Doppler study is a concrete public benchmark, though controlled and not Shahed-specific. ([MDPI][9])

For **acoustic detection networks**, the U.S. Army CALL report on Ukraine’s acoustic monitoring systems is one of the most relevant public discussions: cheap sensors, local ML classification, time synchronization, triangulation, and fusion with air-defense C2. 

## Bottom line

A Shahed-136-class UAV is detectable, but it is difficult because it combines **low altitude, small/variable RCS, slow speed, clutter proximity, long range, cheap mass employment, and saturation tactics**. The limiting factor is often not raw radar sensitivity; it is **line of sight plus clutter plus false-alarm control plus track confirmation**.

For a piston Shahed at roughly **185 km/h**, a ground radar might have only **minutes to tens of minutes** of warning if the drone is low. A high-elevation or airborne sensor can extend that substantially. At the defensive-system level, the best approach is not one radar but a fused network: **elevated radar + local counter-UAS radar + acoustic cueing + EO/IR confirmation + robust C2**.
