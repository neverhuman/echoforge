## Bottom line

Your AUC table is not “too good.” It is **diagnostic of a simulator/evaluation failure**. When CatBoost, LightGBM, CFAR/TBD fusion, a TCN, and a transformer all produce ~1.0 AUC, while the **baseline AUC is already 0.965–0.9995**, the most likely explanation is not that the models solved radar detection. It is that the synthetic data generator is creating a **nearly separable classification problem** through leakage, overly clean priors, deterministic physics, unrealistic clutter/noise, or train/holdout splits drawn from the same narrow generator regimes.

I’ll keep this framed as a **public-proxy, defensive radar-simulation audit**. I’m not providing platform-specific verified signatures, evasion/engagement guidance, or Iran-specific operational performance claims.

# 2. Most likely reason you got AUC ≈ 1.0

The baseline already nearly solves the problem. At the 45 second horizon, your baseline AUC is **0.999531**. The ML lift is only **0.000469**. That means the synthetic classes are almost perfectly separable before deep learning enters the picture.

The highest-probability causes are:

1. **Synthetic positive and negative classes occupy different parameter regions.**
   For example, positive targets may have different SNR, range evolution, Doppler, micro-motion, object-family metadata, scenario priors, or horizon-dependent confidence than hard negatives.

2. **The split is likely random over generated samples, not grouped by physical world.**
   If train/validation/holdout are all sampled from the same generator distributions, same sensor archetypes, same environment priors, same target families, and related seeds, the holdout is not a real holdout. It is interpolation.

3. **The target amplitude is too directly controlled.**
   In the simulator code, target amplitude is derived directly from configured target SNR and AWGN sigma. That bypasses much of the physical chain: radar equation, aspect-dependent RCS, antenna gain, scan loss, range loss, atmospheric loss, clutter-to-noise ratio, receiver dynamic range, and target/clutter coupling. ([GitHub][5])

4. **Noise and clutter are too simple.**
   Real low-altitude small-UAS radar is hard because clutter, multipath, RFI, sidelobes, moving ground objects, birds, foliage, buildings, terrain, and sensor imperfections create target-like artifacts. The literature repeatedly emphasizes clutter, low RCS, bird confusion, terrain proximity, RF interference, and range/Doppler ambiguity as central limitations. ([arXiv][6])

5. **The “horizon” may not be physically represented.**
   Your table reports 5, 15, and 45 second horizons, but the config I found uses sub-second episodes, and the default Rust sim uses 32 pulses with 900 µs PRI, roughly a 28.8 ms coherent processing interval. If your dataset does not model actual multi-second revisit, scan, track-initiation, and temporal evidence accumulation, then “45 seconds” may just be a label/window artifact. ([GitHub][4])

# 3. Ranked list of missing or under-modeled real-world artifacts

## 1. Evaluation leakage and non-independent holdout design

**Impact: catastrophic.** This is likely the number-one reason for AUC 1.0.

Your first fix should not be a better transformer or a more detailed drone model. It should be a brutal leakage audit. A random holdout from the same simulator is not enough. You need holdouts grouped by:

* physical target identity or target parameter family;
* radar sensor archetype;
* frequency band and waveform;
* terrain/land-cover environment;
* clutter regime;
* weather regime;
* simulation seed family;
* generator version;
* scenario template;
* hard-negative family;
* takeoff-site geometry.

A true holdout should contain **entirely unseen worlds**, not merely unseen rows.

Immediate tests:

* Train a **metadata-only model** using no IQ/range-Doppler features. If AUC remains high, labels leak through scenario/object/config fields.
* Train on **target-removed scenes**. If the model still predicts positives, the simulator or labels are leaking.
* Match positive and negative distributions for **SNR, range, radial velocity, altitude, clutter density, RFI, horizon, sensor type, and episode duration**.
* Hold out complete object packs and complete environment packs.
* Shuffle labels within matched scenario buckets. AUC should collapse to chance.
* Train on one generator version and test on another.
* Report results separately for interpolation, extrapolation, and out-of-distribution holdout.

The current repo wisely tracks validation tiers and uncertainty, but the scenario I found is still unvalidated. That means the current benchmark should be treated as synthetic-regression testing, not real-world generalization. ([GitHub][2])

---

## 2. Lack of measured-data anchoring and calibration

**Impact: catastrophic.**

A radar simulator can be useful without measured data, but it cannot honestly claim real-world predictive power without measured-data anchoring. The repo’s own rules correctly state that exact measured-truth claims require lawful measured data and provenance. ([GitHub][1])

You need at least three validation layers:

**Canonical validation:** spheres, plates, trihedrals, cylinders, and analytic scatterers. This validates the solver and radar equation.

**Instrumented range validation:** known objects, known trajectories, known sensor settings, calibrated RCS references, measured noise/clutter backgrounds.

**Operational-environment validation:** low-altitude targets in real clutter, with real sensor logs, real false alarms, real missed detections, and site-held-out testing.

Without this, the simulator will optimize for internal consistency, not real radar behavior. RCS measurement papers emphasize that UAV RCS is frequency-, aspect-, polarization-, material-, and geometry-dependent, and measured distributions are needed to characterize it properly. ([ar5iv][7])

---

## 3. Unrealistic SNR and power-budget modeling

**Impact: catastrophic.**

The simulator appears to set target amplitude directly from configured SNR and AWGN sigma. That is convenient, but it makes the problem too clean. Real radar detectability depends on the radar equation and a chain of losses/gains:

* transmit power;
* antenna gain;
* two-way range loss;
* wavelength;
* target RCS;
* scan loss;
* beam shape;
* polarization mismatch;
* atmospheric loss;
* receiver noise figure;
* system temperature;
* bandwidth;
* processing gain;
* clutter power;
* interference power;
* dynamic range;
* ADC quantization;
* CFAR loss;
* integration loss;
* track-initiation logic.

The config preset I saw uses target SNR in a high range, while real low-altitude small-UAS detection is often clutter-limited rather than thermal-noise-limited. Passive radar literature makes this point strongly: direct-path and multipath clutter can dominate target echoes, and for near-range UAVs the limiting factor can be signal-to-clutter ratio rather than signal-to-noise ratio. ([GitHub][4])

You should replace “assign SNR” with “derive received signal power.” Then compute SNR/SCR/SINR as outputs, not inputs.

---

## 4. Scalar RCS instead of aspect-, frequency-, polarization-, and state-dependent RCS

**Impact: catastrophic.**

Small-UAS RCS is not a scalar. It varies with:

* aspect angle;
* elevation angle;
* frequency;
* polarization;
* bistatic angle;
* airframe material;
* payload;
* landing gear;
* propulsor state;
* control-surface position;
* manufacturing variation;
* attitude;
* engine/propeller state;
* occlusion by terrain/buildings/ground during takeoff.

The literature is very clear that RCS depends on frequency, polarization, viewing angle, object geometry, and material. Measured small-drone RCS can also be statistically modeled with distributions such as generalized extreme value, lognormal, gamma, or related families rather than a fixed scalar. ([arXiv][6])

Your current code appears to use a scalar `rcs_scalar` and a simple scintillation multiplier. That is not enough. You need an RCS model that returns a complex scattering response as a function of:

```text
RCS = f(frequency, polarization_tx, polarization_rx, azimuth, elevation,
        attitude, material_state, component_state, bistatic_geometry, time)
```

For classification experiments, you should also avoid class-separable RCS priors. Hard negatives must overlap with the positive class in RCS, Doppler, speed, altitude, and micro-motion.

---

## 5. Fluctuating-target models and glint are too simple

**Impact: very high.**

Real targets fluctuate. Radar simulation should include pulse-to-pulse and scan-to-scan RCS variation, not just a smooth sinusoidal or scalar multiplier. Common target fluctuation models include Swerling-style models, where RCS can vary across pulses or scans; practical models need to handle fluctuation update intervals and target-type assumptions. ([MathWorks][8])

You should implement:

* Swerling 0/I/II/III/IV-like modes;
* compound fluctuation models;
* aspect-driven glint;
* scattering-center birth/death;
* coherent phase variation;
* non-stationary RCS during rotation/takeoff;
* payload and manufacturing variability;
* scan-to-scan fluctuation;
* correlation between RCS, attitude, and radial velocity.

The key is that glint should arise from geometry and scatterer interference, not just random bright bins.

---

## 6. Clutter is not heavy-tailed, structured, or environment-specific enough

**Impact: very high.**

Real clutter is not Gaussian background fuzz. Land and semi-urban clutter can be heavy-tailed, spatially correlated, Doppler-spread, aspect-dependent, range-dependent, weather-dependent, and nonstationary. Radar clutter literature emphasizes that detection is usually about maximizing SINR, not just SNR, and that clutter mitigation uses degrees of freedom across space, polarization, fast time, slow time, waveform, and resource management. ([its.ntia.gov][9])

Clutter models should include:

* K-distributed clutter;
* Weibull clutter;
* lognormal clutter;
* compound-Gaussian clutter;
* correlated texture/speckle;
* terrain-dependent reflectivity;
* buildings and roof edges;
* roads and vehicles;
* power lines and poles;
* fences;
* foliage and wind-driven motion;
* sea/shoreline if applicable;
* clutter internal motion;
* range-varying clutter maps;
* Doppler-spread ground clutter;
* clutter edges;
* nonhomogeneous CFAR windows.

The literature specifically notes that Gaussian assumptions can be inadequate under high-resolution or low-grazing-angle conditions, and that Weibull, lognormal, and K-distribution clutter are commonly used alternatives. ([PMC][10])

Your current simulator’s correlated clutter and random glints are useful smoke-test ingredients, but they are not a realistic clutter field.

## 7. Low-altitude takeoff multipath is under-modeled

**Impact: very high.**

A ground-launched, low-altitude object is exactly where multipath matters most. Direct-path and reflected-path returns can constructively or destructively interfere. Multipath can create:

* range ghosts;
* angle bias;
* elevation errors;
* fading;
* apparent acceleration artifacts;
* false tracks;
* track swaps;
* detection dropouts;
* glints from ground/buildings/vehicles.

Low-angle radar literature describes direct and indirect paths, specular and diffuse reflection components, angle errors, random diffuse variation, and power drops caused by interference. 

A second multipath reference emphasizes that specular and diffuse multipath can add constructively or destructively, making the received signal stronger, weaker, or nearly vanish; this directly affects target detection and tracking. 

Your simulator should include:

```text
direct path
+ specular ground bounce
+ diffuse ground reflection
+ building-wall reflection
+ roof-edge scattering
+ vehicle reflection
+ terrain-shadowed path
+ antenna sidelobe reception
+ time-varying phase interference
```

This is probably one of the biggest missing pieces for a takeoff scenario.

A realistic takeoff simulation needs an actual 3D scene model:

The UAV radar survey literature specifically notes that low-flying UAVs are hard because they can fly close to terrain and be immersed in clutter; it also notes that clutter can include buildings, man-made structures, birds, trees, and hills. ([arXiv][6])

A “takeoff” scenario without ground interaction, local occlusion, and low-grazing-angle clutter is not a real takeoff scenario.

# 5. Highest-impact rebuild plan

## Phase 0 — Break the benchmark on purpose

Do this before adding physics.

1. Metadata-only classifier.
2. Target-removed classifier.
3. SNR/range/velocity-matched evaluation.

|    5 | Heavy-tailed clutter              | Gaussian/simple clutter misses target-like outliers.                             |
|    6 | Low-angle multipath               | Takeoff geometry creates fading, ghosts, and angle/range errors.                 |
|    7 | Full complex IQ processing        | Magnitude-only proxies distort Doppler/micro-Doppler behavior.                   |
|    8 | Antenna/scan/revisit model        | Multi-second horizons require realistic sensor scheduling.                       |
|    9 | Hardware impairments              | Real receivers have phase noise, IQ imbalance, compression, quantization, drift. |
|   10 | Hard-negative overlap             | Birds, vehicles, turbines, glints, and RFI must overlap positives.               |
|   11 | CFAR/tracker realism              | CA-CFAR on easy synthetic profiles is not enough.                                |
|   12 | Operational metrics               | Use Pd/Pfa, false tracks/hour, time-to-detect, calibration, OOD performance.     |

---

# 7. Final diagnosis

Your simulator is a promising scaffold, but the current benchmark appears to be measuring **synthetic separability**, not real-world radar detection. The strongest evidence is in your own table: the baseline nearly solves the task, and every model family reaches perfection. That almost never happens in realistic low-altitude radar detection unless the task is leaking labels or the world is too clean.

The most important next move is not to add model complexity. It is to make the simulator meaner:

* matched positives and negatives;
* grouped OOD holdouts;
* true multi-second radar timelines;
* measured-data anchoring;
* complex-IQ processing;
* heavy-tailed clutter;
* low-angle multipath;
* real RCS variability;
* calibrated false-alarm metrics.

Once those are in place, expect AUC to drop sharply. That drop would be a **good sign**: it would mean EchoForge is finally starting to simulate the hard parts of radar rather than giving the models an answer key.
