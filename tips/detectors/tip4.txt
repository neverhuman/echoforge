# Public-source technical report: military radar/sensor systems used against Shahed-type drones

Below is a public-source technical report on radar and sensor systems used to detect low-flying one-way attack drones such as the Shahed-136 / Geran-2 class. I’m treating “radar detectors” as **detection radars and sensor systems**, not police-radar-warning receivers. I’m also keeping this at the public technical/specification level: I am not including classified receiver thresholds, exact waveform libraries, live deployment geometry, or tactical exploitation/evasion guidance.

## 1. Executive findings

**Shahed-136-class drones are best detected by a layered sensor stack, not by one radar type.** Publicly documented systems combine long/medium-range 3D surveillance radars, short-range C-UAS AESA radars, EO/IR cameras, passive RF sensors, acoustic arrays, and command-and-control fusion. That is because a Shahed-type drone sits between two categories: it is larger than a quadcopter, but it can still be low-altitude, relatively slow, propeller-driven, and difficult to separate from ground clutter, birds, weather, terrain returns, and other low-speed objects.

**The most important published “sensitivity” data is usually not receiver sensitivity in dBm.** Military vendors rarely publish minimum detectable signal, probability-of-detection curves, false-alarm rate, noise figure, waveform details, or detection range versus exact radar cross-section. Instead, public specifications usually give proxies: target classes, instrumented range, track range, update rate, track capacity, elevation coverage, frequency band, AESA/PESA architecture, and detection examples such as “nano UAV at 5 km” or “fighter at 120 km.”

**The United States’ clearest public C-UAS architecture is LIDS / FS-LIDS**, built around Raytheon’s KuRFS or KuMRFS radar, FAAD C2, SRC electronic warfare components, EO/IR, and Coyote interceptors. RTX describes LIDS as designed for Group 1–3 UAS and cruise-missile-like attack systems, with KuRFS able to detect very small fast objects and discriminate targets from clutter. ([RTX][1])

**Ukraine’s Shahed defense is unusually sensor-fused.** Public sources describe Ukraine using Western radars such as HENSOLDT TRML-4D and SPEXER, Thales GM200, Saab Giraffe, and large passive acoustic networks that cue mobile teams and air defense units. HENSOLDT says TRML-4D detects and tracks cruise missiles, drones, aircraft, and helicopters out to a radar radius of up to 250 km, while its SPEXER family is built for ground, sea, and low-flying air targets. ([HENSOLDT][2])

**Russia fields layered low-altitude and SHORAD radars**, including Pantsir-S1, Tor-M2E, Podlet-K1-type low-altitude surveillance radars, Kasta/Nebo families, and other acquisition radars. Rosoboronexport states Tor-M2E is intended for aircraft, helicopters, UAVs, guided missiles, and precision weapons at medium, low, and extremely low altitude, with a C-band acquisition radar and K-band guidance station. ([Rostec Export][3])

**China has a large, export-oriented air-defense radar ecosystem** that includes low-altitude/counter-drone radars such as JY-11, YLC-12, YLC-18, JH-18, “3G Mesh,” and integrated anti-UAV systems such as AUICS-3000, plus longer-range/counter-stealth families such as JY-26, JY-27A, YLC-8, and SLC-7. A CASI review of China’s radar industrial base says PLA mobile radar systems cover ranges up to roughly 650 km, with low-altitude gaps addressed by systems such as YLC-18. ([Air University][4])

# 2. The target: Shahed-136 / Geran-2 as a radar problem

Public descriptions of Shahed-136 vary by source and variant. RUSI describes Shahed-136 as a scaled-up Shahed-131, with a **wingspan of about 2.5 m**, **length of about 3.5 m**, and an estimated range of **1,350–1,500 km**. RUSI also notes carbon-fiber / honeycomb construction and a piston engine architecture. ([RUSI][5])

A useful defensive radar classification is:

| Attribute                   | Why it matters to radar/sensor detection                                                                                                                                    |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Small-to-moderate airframe  | Larger than quadcopters, but still much smaller than conventional aircraft. Detection range depends heavily on radar cross-section, aspect angle, frequency, and materials. |
| Low altitude                | Reduces line-of-sight range, increases ground clutter, and can place the target in terrain/urban/vegetation multipath.                                                      |
| Propeller / piston engine   | Gives acoustic and micro-Doppler signatures that can help classification, but also creates a low-speed Doppler problem compared with jets or missiles.                      |
| One-way attack profile      | Defenders need early warning, track continuity, classification, handoff, and engagement-quality track data under time pressure.                                             |
| Composite / mixed materials | Radar cross-section is not simply determined by physical size; material, geometry, frequency, polarization, and aspect all matter.                                          |

Newer Russian/Iranian-family variants complicate the picture. CSIS describes a jet-powered Geran-3 as having a reported range of about **2,500 km** and speed of **550–600 km/h**, which shifts the detection/intercept problem closer to a small cruise-missile profile. ([CSIS][6])

# 3. Radar science behind detecting low-flying drones

## 3.1 Radar equation and “sensitivity”

For a monostatic radar, the received echo power is often summarized as:

[
P_r = \frac{P_t G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma}{(4\pi)^3 R^4 L}
]

Where:

| Term      | Meaning                                                                              |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| (P_r)     | received echo power                                                                  |
| (P_t)     | transmit power                                                                       |
| (G)       | antenna gain                                                                         |
| (\lambda) | wavelength                                                                           |
| (\sigma)  | target radar cross-section                                                           |
| (R)       | range                                                                                |
| (L)       | losses: propagation, atmosphere, hardware, processing, polarization, scan loss, etc. |

The key practical point is the **fourth-power range law**: doubling detection range requires much more than doubling power. If target radar cross-section shrinks by 100×, maximum detection range falls by about (100^{1/4}), or roughly 3.16×, all else equal. This is why small drones force radars into high-gain antennas, high update rates, coherent integration, Doppler processing, clutter rejection, and sensor fusion rather than just “more power.”

Public sources describe the radar receiver chain as a combination of ADC, low-noise amplifier, mixer, filter, matched filter, threshold detector, coherent integration, and Doppler/micro-Doppler processing. The same survey notes that range depends on transmit power, antenna gain, minimum received power, system losses, thermal noise, noise bandwidth, and integration. ([arXiv][7])

## 3.2 Minimum detectable signal is not a fixed number

A radar’s “sensitivity” is not just a receiver number like `-110 dBm`. It depends on:

| Factor                            | Effect                                                                                                             |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Receiver noise figure             | Determines how much thermal noise is added by the receiver.                                                        |
| Bandwidth                         | Wider bandwidth improves range resolution but increases thermal noise.                                             |
| Processing gain                   | Coherent integration, pulse compression, Doppler filtering, and track-before-detect improve weak-target detection. |
| Required probability of detection | Higher detection probability generally requires a higher threshold.                                                |
| False alarm requirement           | Lower false alarm rate requires stricter thresholding, which can reduce sensitivity.                               |
| Clutter                           | In low-altitude drone detection, clutter can dominate thermal noise.                                               |
| Target RCS and aspect             | A drone’s radar return changes with viewing angle, frequency, polarization, and materials.                         |
| Scan rate and dwell time          | Fast revisit rates improve track continuity but may reduce dwell/integration per beam.                             |
| Multipath                         | Low-altitude targets over ground, water, urban structures, or hills can produce fading and false tracks.           |

A radar can be “sensitive” in a clean anechoic model and still struggle in low-angle clutter. Modern C-UAS radars therefore emphasize **CFAR thresholding**, **Doppler filtering**, **micro-Doppler classification**, **track-while-scan**, **multi-beam AESA scanning**, and **sensor fusion**.

## 3.3 Radar cross-section and why drones are hard

Radar cross-section depends on frequency, polarization, view angle, geometry, and material. UAV detection is difficult because many small drones have small dimensions, complex geometry, non-metallic structures, and low-altitude flight profiles; birds and other natural objects can appear in the same speed/altitude region. ([arXiv][7])

A Shahed-type drone is not as tiny as a quadcopter, but it still presents several hard features:

* It is often **low-altitude**, which pushes the radar into ground clutter and horizon limits.
* Its speed can overlap with helicopters, birds, small aircraft, and some ground-multipath artifacts.
* Its airframe and aspect can produce variable radar cross-section.
* Its propeller and engine can provide classification clues, but classification requires enough dwell time, signal quality, and processing.

## 3.4 Doppler, micro-Doppler, and classification

Conventional Doppler separates moving targets from stationary clutter by radial velocity. Drones can be difficult because their ground speed and radial velocity may be low or variable. Micro-Doppler analysis looks at rotating propellers, rotors, and vibration signatures to distinguish UAVs from birds or other clutter. The radar survey specifically identifies Doppler and micro-Doppler as major features for UAV classification and notes that machine learning is commonly used for target classification. ([arXiv][7])

For a Shahed-like target, useful radar observables include:

| Observable                  | Defensive use                                                           |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Range / bearing / elevation | Track formation and cueing.                                             |
| Radial velocity             | Separation from clutter; trajectory estimate.                           |
| Micro-Doppler               | Propeller/engine-related classification evidence.                       |
| RCS fluctuation             | Helps track/classification but can also complicate continuity.          |
| Track behavior              | Route, altitude, speed consistency, and correlation with other sensors. |
| Sensor cross-correlation    | Radar + EO/IR + acoustic + RF reduces false positives.                  |

## 3.5 Low-elevation radar issues

Low elevation is one of the hardest cases because of:

| Problem        | What happens                                                                                                                                            |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Radar horizon  | Curvature and terrain limit line-of-sight. Higher radar placement and target altitude increase range, but exact coverage depends on terrain and siting. |
| Ground clutter | Buildings, trees, hills, roads, towers, vehicles, animals, and terrain reflections create strong returns.                                               |
| Multipath      | The radar receives direct and reflected paths, causing fading, ghosting, or distorted elevation estimates.                                              |
| Weather        | Rain, snow, fog, humidity, and ducting can alter attenuation, clutter, and propagation.                                                                 |
| Birds          | Bird flocks can produce moving low-altitude tracks with confusing Doppler signatures.                                                                   |
| Wind turbines  | Blade micro-Doppler and high RCS can generate strong clutter and false tracks.                                                                          |
| Urban clutter  | Corners, rooftops, masts, power lines, and vehicles increase false alarms and multipath.                                                                |

The UAV radar survey explicitly calls out clutter from buildings, birds, trees, hills, precipitation, and clutter cross-section that can be far larger than a small target’s radar cross-section. It also notes UAV radar limitations from small RCS, terrain-hugging flight, Doppler ambiguity, swarms, adaptive thresholding, ECM, RF noise, and clutter. ([arXiv][7])

# 4. Major radar and sensor types used for Shahed-like detection

## 4.1 Long/medium-range 3D air-surveillance radars

These are used for wide-area early warning and air-defense cueing. They usually operate in S-band, C-band, L-band, or similar bands, with rotating or electronically scanned arrays.

**Strengths:** wide coverage, long range, many tracks, integration with air-defense C2.
**Limits:** small low-altitude targets can be masked by terrain/clutter; update rate may be lower than short-range C-UAS radars; not every track is engagement-quality.

Examples: HENSOLDT TRML-4D, Thales GM200, Saab Giraffe AMB/4A, Northrop Grumman G/ATOR, Russian Nebo/Kasta/Podlet families, Chinese JY/YLC/SLC families.

## 4.2 Short-range C-UAS AESA radars

These are optimized for low, slow, small targets. They often use X-band, Ku-band, or S-band AESA panels, sometimes arranged in multi-panel 360° configurations.

**Strengths:** higher resolution, fast revisit, better low-altitude track continuity, small-drone classification.
**Limits:** shorter range, line-of-sight limits, requires dense coverage or cueing from larger radars.

Examples: RTX KuRFS / KuMRFS, Leonardo DRS RADA MHR/nMHR, HENSOLDT SPEXER, Saab Giraffe 1X, Echodyne EchoShield/EchoGuard, Rafael Drone Dome radars.

## 4.3 C-RAM / counter-fire / multimission radars

These radars were designed or adapted to detect rockets, artillery, mortars, and fast small objects. Many have been adapted for C-UAS.

**Strengths:** high precision, rapid track formation, small-object detection, fire-control integration.
**Limits:** sector/mission tradeoffs, waveform/tasking tradeoffs, potential overload in dense environments.

Examples: KuRFS, AN/TPQ-50, Q-53, MHR/nMHR, GM200 MM/C.

## 4.4 Passive radar

Passive radar does not transmit. It uses illuminators of opportunity such as FM radio, digital radio, or TV broadcast signals and analyzes reflections from targets.

**Strengths:** hard to detect because it emits no radar signal; useful as a gap-filler; can exploit VHF/UHF wavelengths; resilient in some jamming contexts.
**Limits:** depends on available illuminators, geometry, processing, and target/reflection conditions; less direct control over waveform.

HENSOLDT’s Twinvis passive radar uses existing radio/TV transmitters, does not require the target to emit, and provides 360° passive 3D tracking with sub-second update behavior in public specifications. ([HENSOLDT][8])

## 4.5 Passive RF / SIGINT sensors

Passive RF systems detect and classify command links, telemetry, video links, navigation emissions, or other RF signatures. Against fully autonomous one-way attack drones, RF detection can be less reliable than radar/acoustic/EO because the drone may emit little or nothing, but RF sensors are valuable against many commercial and tactical UAS.

## 4.6 EO/IR cameras

Electro-optical and infrared sensors are generally used for identification, classification, fire-control support, and operator confirmation. They are line-of-sight and weather/visibility limited, but they help reduce false positives.

## 4.7 Acoustic networks

Acoustic sensors are not radar, but they are highly relevant to Shahed detection because piston/propeller one-way drones can have recognizable acoustic signatures. Public reporting on Ukraine describes low-cost passive acoustic networks using microphones or microphone arrays, edge processing, and sensor fusion to detect and cue defenses against low-altitude UAS. The same source notes that small-UAS low RCS, low altitude, and slow speed complicate legacy radar, which is why acoustic cueing can be useful. ([Army API][9])

# 5. Publicly documented systems and specifications

## 5.1 United States and U.S.-allied systems

### RTX / Raytheon KuRFS, KuMRFS, LIDS, FS-LIDS

**Role:** U.S. Army low, slow, small UAS detection and defeat; C-UAS and C-RAM support.
**Architecture:** Ku-band electronically scanned radar integrated with FAAD C2, EO/IR, electronic warfare, and Coyote interceptors in LIDS / FS-LIDS.
**Public details:** RTX states that KuRFS and Coyote are central detect/defeat components of the U.S. Army’s Low, slow, small UAS Integrated Defeat System. RTX also says KuRFS can detect objects as small as a 9 mm bullet, discriminate targets from clutter, and support fixed, relocatable, and mobile configurations. ([RTX][1])

The U.S. Defense Security Cooperation Agency’s 2026 UAE FS-LIDS notification lists ten FS-LIDS systems, Coyote Block 2 interceptors, KuMRFS radars, EO/IR cameras, FAAD C2, and support equipment. It describes KuMRFS as a multifunction radar that provides simultaneous C-UAS and C-RAM air surveillance, 3D target location, and effector support. ([Federal Register][10])

**Why it matters for Shahed-type targets:** This is one of the clearest public examples of a modern U.S. C-UAS architecture: radar detection, C2 fusion, EO/IR, EW, and kinetic effectors designed for small UAS and cruise-missile-like threats.

---

### AN/MPQ-64 Sentinel

**Role:** Short-to-medium-range 3D air-defense radar.
**Targets:** Aircraft, helicopters, drones, missiles, cruise-missile-type threats.
**Public details:** Raytheon describes Sentinel as a 3D radar that reports range, bearing, and elevation, supports C2 and fire-control data, and is used against UAS, cruise missiles, fixed-wing aircraft, and rotary-wing aircraft. Raytheon says more than 300 Sentinel radars are deployed worldwide by the U.S. Army and allies. ([RTX][11])

**Why it matters:** Sentinel is a mature U.S./allied short-range air-defense radar that can feed air-defense systems and C2 networks.

---

### Leonardo DRS / RADA MHR

**Role:** Ground-based multimission radar for C-UAS, VSHORAD, C-RAM, and hemispheric surveillance.
**Band / architecture:** S-band, pulse-Doppler, software-defined, AESA, GaN.
**Public performance examples:** Leonardo DRS lists maximum detection ranges of **5 km for nano UAVs**, **25 km for medium UAVs**, **50 km for heavy transport aircraft**, **35 km for fighters**, **20 km for low-RCS fighters**, and **23 km for utility helicopters**. It also lists 90° azimuth / 90° elevation per radar and 360° coverage with four radars. ([DRS RADA Technologies][12])

**Why it matters:** MHR is representative of compact AESA C-UAS radars used for tactical air defense and mobile protection.

---

### Leonardo DRS / RADA nMHR

**Role:** Higher-performance multimission radar for C-UAS, VSHORAD, C-RAM, and hemispheric surveillance.
**Band / architecture:** X-band, pulse-Doppler, software-defined, AESA, GaN, digital beamforming.
**Public performance examples:** Leonardo DRS lists maximum detection ranges of **8 km for nano UAVs**, **36 km for medium UAVs**, **80 km for heavy transport aircraft**, **52 km for fighters**, **28 km for low-RCS fighters**, and **36 km for utility helicopters**. It also lists 90° azimuth / 90° elevation per radar and 360° hemispheric coverage with four radars. ([DRS RADA Technologies][13])

**Why it matters:** nMHR is a strong public benchmark for tactical AESA C-UAS sensitivity by target class.

---

### SRC AN/TPQ-50

**Role:** Lightweight counterfire radar adapted for air surveillance and C-UAS.
**Architecture:** 360° surveillance, electronically steered, non-rotating antenna.
**Public performance examples:** SRC describes TPQ-50 as a U.S. Army program-of-record radar that supports both weapons location and air surveillance. It lists C-RAM range up to **15 km**, point-of-origin accuracy of **50 m at 10 km**, and air-mode tracking of aircraft including small UAS with **instrumented range greater than 35 km**. ([SRC][14])

**Why it matters:** TPQ-50 shows how counterfire radars can be extended into small-UAS air surveillance.

---

### Lockheed Martin Q-53

**Role:** Counterfire radar adapted to counter-UAS and multimission sensing.
**Public details:** Lockheed Martin says Q-53 was integrated with U.S. Army command-and-control at Yuma Proving Ground to provide C-UAS tracking data and act as a primary fire-control source for Coyote Block 2 through FAAD C2. Lockheed also states it delivered 195 Q-53 radars to the U.S. Army and partners. ([Media - Lockheed Martin][15])

Lockheed later described Q-53 MMR integration with Anduril Lattice during a CENTCOM exercise, pushing radar data into a common air picture for drone-threat detection and tracking. ([Media - Lockheed Martin][16])

**Why it matters:** Q-53 illustrates the trend of repurposing high-quality counterfire radars into C-UAS networks.

### Northrop Grumman AN/TPS-80 G/ATOR

**Role:** USMC / USAF ground/air task-oriented radar for air/missile defense, fire control, and air traffic control.
**Band / architecture:** S-band, GaN AESA, long-range, 4D, 360° surveillance.
**Public details:** Northrop Grumman states G/ATOR provides single-scan fire-control-quality tracks and is intended to detect threats including cruise missiles, hypersonic missiles, ballistic missiles, manned aircraft, and UAVs. ([Northrop Grumman][17])

**Why it matters:** G/ATOR is a broader air-defense radar rather than a small C-UAS-only radar, but it is relevant to Shahed-type detection as part of a layered air-defense network.

### Echodyne EchoShield / EchoGuard

**Role:** Compact electronically scanned radar for C-UAS, perimeter security, and mobile sensing.
**Architecture:** Low-SWaP electronically scanned array; cognitive radar features.
**Public details:** Echodyne lists EchoShield accuracy of **less than 0.5° azimuth/elevation** and range accuracy from **0.75–6 m depending on waveform**, with **10 Hz track quality** across hundreds of objects and AI/ML classification for humans, vehicles, fixed-wing aircraft, and multirotors. ([Echodyne][18])

Echodyne’s C-UAS page states its systems are used by defense customers, including every branch of the U.S. armed forces and allied forces, and gives typical tracking ranges by UAS group. ([Echodyne][19])

**Why it matters:** Echodyne is a good example of modern compact, software-heavy ESA radar designed for persistent low-altitude monitoring.

**Sensor stack:** Phased-array guidance station and target acquisition radar.
**Public performance:** Rosoboronexport says Tor-M2E is intended to destroy aircraft, helicopters, UAVs, guided missiles, and precision weapons at medium, low, and extremely low altitudes in difficult air and interference conditions. Public specs list a C-band acquisition radar with **32 km** detection range, 360° azimuth coverage, and tracking of **48 targets per antenna rotation**, plus a K-band guidance station. ([Rostec Export][3])

**Why it matters:** Tor is explicitly described as a low/extremely-low-altitude SHORAD system and is therefore relevant to drone and cruise-missile-like targets.

---

### Podlet-K1 / 48Ya6-K1 low-altitude radar

**Role:** Low-altitude surveillance radar associated with Russian layered air defense.
**Public details:** Open-source reporting describes Podlet-K1 as an S-band phased-array radar optimized for low and very-low-altitude targets, reportedly used with S-300PMU-2 / S-400 architectures and tracking up to about 200 targets depending on source. Reported detection ranges vary by target, altitude, and environment. ([The War Zone][32])

**Why it matters:** This is the class of radar Russia uses to reduce low-altitude gaps around long-range SAM systems.

---

### Russian broader radar families

Rosoboronexport’s public catalog groups Russian air-target detection radars across meter, decimeter, and centimeter bands and includes systems such as 1L125E, 55Zh6ME/UME, 59N6-TE, Nebo-SVU, Prima, and Resonance-NE. ([Rostec Export][33])

**Why it matters:** Russia’s approach is layered: long-range surveillance/counter-stealth radars, low-altitude gap-filler radars, SHORAD acquisition radars, and EO/radar fire-control systems.

## 5.6 China

### JY-11 low-altitude surveillance radar

**Role:** Low-altitude / counter-drone surveillance radar.
**Public details:** Chinese official reporting describes JY-11 as an S-band, three-face-array low-altitude surveillance radar for counter-drone missions, using a digital array and software-defined architecture. It is described as detecting and tracking fighter jets, low-altitude cruise missiles, and micro/small drones, and as capable of cueing EO and precision radars. ([NCSTI][34])

**Why it matters:** JY-11 is one of the clearest Chinese public examples of a radar explicitly positioned for low-altitude counter-drone and cruise-missile-like threats.

---

### YLC-12, YLC-18, JH-18, AUICS-3000

**Role:** Low-to-medium altitude and anti-UAV surveillance / integrated C-UAS.
**Public details:** Chinese official reporting describes YLC-12 as a C-band low-to-medium-altitude multirole radar for low-flying, slow, highly maneuverable targets with anti-jamming capability. CASI identifies CETC products including AUICS-3000 integrated anti-UAV systems, JH-18 low-altitude surveillance radar, and YLC-18 low-altitude 3D radar. ([NCSTI][34])

**Why it matters:** These are the kinds of systems China markets and fields to cover low-altitude gaps and defend against UAVs.

---

### JY-26, JY-27A, YLC-8, SLC-7, YLC-20/JY-50 passive systems

**Role:** Long-range surveillance, counter-stealth, passive detection, and integrated air defense.
**Public details:** Chinese official reporting describes JY-26 as having a detection range of **500 km**, JY-27A as a counter-stealth radar, YLC-8E as a UHF anti-stealth radar, and SLC-7 as tracking a wide range of targets from stealth aircraft to artillery. CASI identifies JY-50 and YLC-20 as passive/direction-finding radar products. ([NCSTI][34])

**Why it matters:** These systems are less “Shahed-specific” and more part of China’s broad national / theater air-defense sensor network.

# 7. What the U.S., UAE, China, Russia, Ukraine, and others use

## United States

The U.S. public architecture is layered:

1. **LIDS / FS-LIDS** for low, slow, small UAS defense, using KuRFS/KuMRFS radar, FAAD C2, EO/IR, EW, and Coyote interceptors. RTX and DSCA sources describe this as the central U.S. Army C-UAS architecture for Group 1–3 UAS and cruise-missile-like systems. ([RTX][1])
2. **Sentinel AN/MPQ-64** for 3D SHORAD radar coverage against drones, missiles, helicopters, and aircraft. ([RTX][11])
3. **Q-53 and TPQ-50** as counterfire radars adapted to C-UAS / air-surveillance roles. ([Media - Lockheed Martin][15])
4. **G/ATOR** as a Marine Corps / Air Force long-range multifunction radar with UAV and cruise missile coverage. ([Northrop Grumman][17])
5. **Compact C-UAS radars** such as MHR/nMHR and Echodyne systems for local coverage, vehicle-mounted defense, and low-altitude gap filling. ([DRS RADA Technologies][12])

## UAE

The UAE appears to be combining imported U.S. systems with domestic C-UAS development.

1. **FS-LIDS procurement**: DSCA’s 2026 notification lists ten FS-LIDS systems with KuMRFS radars, EO/IR cameras, Coyote Block 2 interceptors, FAAD C2, and related components. ([Federal Register][10])
2. **EDGE SkyShield**: UAE’s EDGE describes SkyShield as a radar/EO/RF/countermeasure C-UAS system with detection, identification, GNSS/C2 jamming, spoofing, and networked C2. ([EDGE][30])

## Ukraine

Ukraine’s public approach is highly fused and adaptive:

1. **Western medium-range radars**: HENSOLDT TRML-4D, Thales GM200, Saab Giraffe-family radars, and other NATO-supplied radars provide wide-area detection and air-defense cueing. ([HENSOLDT][2])
2. **Short-range low-altitude radars**: HENSOLDT SPEXER and Saab Giraffe 1X-type systems address low-altitude small-target coverage. ([HENSOLDT][21])
3. **Acoustic detection networks**: Public analysis describes Ukraine using passive acoustic networks such as Sky Fortress, Zvook, and FENEK to detect/cue defenses against low-altitude UAS, especially where radar coverage is limited or expensive. ([Army API][9])
4. **Sensor fusion**: Radar, acoustic, EO/IR, and command-and-control fusion are central, because Shahed-like drones are numerous, low-flying, and comparatively cheap.

## Russia

Russia’s public systems include:

1. **Tor-M2E** for mobile low/extremely-low-altitude SHORAD against aircraft, helicopters, UAVs, guided missiles, and precision weapons. ([Rostec Export][3])
2. **Pantsir-S1** for point defense with acquisition radar, tracking/guidance radar, EO, missiles, and guns. ([Rostec Export][31])
3. **Low-altitude surveillance radars** such as Podlet-K1 and cataloged Russian radar families including Nebo, Kasta, Resonance, and others. ([The War Zone][32])

## China

China fields and exports a large radar ecosystem:

1. **JY-11** for low-altitude counter-drone and cruise-missile surveillance. ([NCSTI][34])
2. **YLC-12, YLC-18, JH-18, AUICS-3000** for low-altitude, low-to-medium-altitude, and integrated anti-UAV roles. ([NCSTI][34])
3. **JY-26, JY-27A, YLC-8E, SLC-7** for longer-range, counter-stealth, and broader integrated air-defense sensing. ([NCSTI][34])
4. **Passive / direction-finding systems** such as JY-50 and YLC-20, identified in CASI’s survey of Chinese radar manufacturers. ([Air University][4])

## Israel and Europe

Israel and Europe are major suppliers of C-UAS and air-defense radar:

1. **Rafael Drone Dome** integrates radar, RF, EO, jamming, and C2; public brochures list RPS-42 and RPS-82 radar ranges in the 5–10 km class. ([Rafael][27])
2. **HENSOLDT TRML-4D and SPEXER** cover medium-range air defense and short-range C-UAS/gap-filling roles. ([HENSOLDT][20])
3. **Thales GM200 / GM200 MM/C** cover medium-range air surveillance, SHORAD coordination, UAS, missiles, RAM, and weapon locating. ([Thales Group][22])
4. **Saab Giraffe 1X / AMB / 4A** span lightweight C-UAS gap filling through long-range multifunction radar. ([Start][24])

# 8. What “best detection methods” look like in practice

For Shahed-type threats, the strongest public pattern is:

## 8.1 Early warning layer

Medium/long-range 3D radars detect and form initial tracks. Examples include TRML-4D, GM200, Giraffe AMB/4A, G/ATOR, and Russian/Chinese theater radars. This layer provides the longest warning time but may not always provide the best small-target classification at low altitude.

## 8.2 Low-altitude gap layer

Short-range AESA radars fill local low-altitude gaps around protected assets. Examples include KuRFS/KuMRFS, MHR/nMHR, SPEXER, Giraffe 1X, EchoShield, and Drone Dome radars. This layer has better resolution and faster revisit for low/slow/small objects.

## 8.3 Passive detection layer

Acoustic, passive RF, and passive radar systems cue active sensors and reduce radar-only false alarms. Ukraine’s acoustic networks are a major public example of this approach for Shahed-like drones. ([Army API][9])

## 8.4 Identification layer

EO/IR cameras and RF classification help confirm whether a radar track is a drone, bird, aircraft, weather artifact, or ground-clutter artifact.

## 8.5 C2 / fusion layer

Systems such as FAAD C2, SkyShield C2, Drone Dome C4I, and national air-defense networks fuse sensors and assign effectors. The U.S. FS-LIDS notification specifically describes FAAD C2 receiving alerts from radar, RF detectors, and external sources before activating kinetic or non-kinetic options. ([Federal Register][10])

# 9. Common false positives and noise concerns

## 9.1 Radar false positives

| False-positive source        | Why it looks like a drone                                                 |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Birds / bird flocks          | Low altitude, moving returns, sometimes similar speed range.              |
| Wind turbines                | Strong radar cross-section and blade micro-Doppler.                       |
| Ground vehicles              | Multipath or partial line-of-sight can create low-elevation tracks.       |
| Buildings and towers         | Static clutter and multipath reflections.                                 |
| Trees / vegetation           | Wind-driven movement produces Doppler clutter.                            |
| Weather                      | Rain, snow, fog, and anomalous propagation create clutter or attenuation. |
| Small aircraft / helicopters | Legitimate flying objects requiring classification and deconfliction.     |
| Sea clutter                  | Strong time-varying low-elevation returns near coastlines.                |
| Insects / biological clutter | Can matter for sensitive short-range radars.                              |

The UAV radar survey identifies clutter from buildings, birds, trees, hills, precipitation, and other objects as a central obstacle, and notes that clutter cross-section can be much larger than the target radar cross-section. ([arXiv][7])

## 9.2 Signal and processing problems

| Problem                    | Impact                                                                                  |
| -------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Low SNR                    | Weak target returns require more integration or lower thresholds, raising false alarms. |
| Low radial velocity        | Target can fall near clutter notch or be harder to distinguish from slow clutter.       |
| Aspect-dependent RCS       | Track quality may fluctuate as the target turns or geometry changes.                    |
| Multipath fading           | Track intermittency, ghost tracks, or elevation errors.                                 |
| Track fragmentation        | One target appears as multiple short tracks.                                            |
| Dense swarms / mass raids  | Track association and C2 load become difficult.                                         |
| Jamming / interference     | Raises noise floor or disrupts tracking/classification.                                 |
| Classification uncertainty | Radar track exists, but type is ambiguous without EO/IR, RF, or acoustic confirmation.  |

Modern radars address these problems with AESA multi-beam scanning, adaptive thresholding, Doppler and micro-Doppler classification, track-before-detect, sensor fusion, and AI/ML classification. The survey notes GaN, compact antennas, phased arrays, efficient signal processing, lower false alarm rates, clutter discrimination, and multi-target processing as key modern radar developments. ([arXiv][7])

# 10. Practical meaning of published detection ranges

A vendor statement like “medium UAV at 25 km” or “small target at 3.5 km” is useful, but it does not mean every Shahed-like target will be detected at that range in every environment. Public range claims are affected by:

| Factor             | Why the same radar can perform differently                                        |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Target RCS         | A quadcopter, Shahed, cruise missile, and bird differ greatly.                    |
| Aspect angle       | Nose-on, side-on, and tail-on returns can differ.                                 |
| Altitude           | Low altitude reduces line of sight and increases clutter.                         |
| Terrain            | Hills, urban canyons, vegetation, and water change performance.                   |
| Radar siting       | Height, masking, horizon, and clutter environment dominate low-altitude coverage. |
| Weather            | Rain, snow, humidity, and ducting can change SNR and clutter.                     |
| Mode               | Search mode, track mode, C-RAM mode, and engagement mode may differ.              |
| False-alarm policy | A stricter threshold reduces false alarms but can reduce weak-target detection.   |
| Network fusion     | Multiple sensors can detect what one radar misses.                                |

This is why the best defensive systems do not rely on a single published range number. They build a **track-quality chain**: detect → confirm → classify → maintain track → hand off → engage or alert.

# 11. System-level architecture for detecting Shahed-type drones

A robust public-source architecture looks like this:

1. **Wide-area radar layer**
   TRML-4D / GM200 / Giraffe AMB or 4A / G/ATOR / national air-defense radars.

2. **Local low-altitude radar layer**
   KuRFS / KuMRFS / MHR / nMHR / SPEXER / Giraffe 1X / EchoShield / Drone Dome radar.

3. **Passive layer**
   Acoustic arrays, passive RF detectors, passive radar, visual observers, civilian reports where appropriate.

4. **Identification layer**
   EO/IR cameras, thermal imagers, RF fingerprinting, acoustic classification, micro-Doppler classification.

5. **C2 fusion layer**
   FAAD C2, Lattice-like common air picture systems, national air-defense C2, local mobile-team alerting tools.

6. **Effector layer**
   Not the focus of this report, but public systems pair sensors with interceptors, guns, missiles, EW, jammers, or mobile response teams.

For Shahed-like drones, the most important design principle is **not maximum range alone**. It is **continuous track custody through low-altitude clutter** with enough classification confidence to avoid wasting interceptors or engaging false targets.

---

# 12. Bottom-line ranking by role, not by “best radar”

There is no single “best” radar for Shahed-type drones. The right answer depends on the defended area, terrain, budget, threat density, and available effectors. Publicly, the strongest systems by role are:

| Role                                         | Strong public examples                                              |
| -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| U.S. integrated C-UAS                        | LIDS / FS-LIDS with KuRFS or KuMRFS, FAAD C2, EO/IR, EW, Coyote.    |
| Medium-range Ukraine-style air-defense radar | TRML-4D, GM200, Giraffe AMB/4A.                                     |
| Short-range low-altitude C-UAS radar         | KuRFS/KuMRFS, nMHR/MHR, SPEXER, Giraffe 1X, EchoShield, Drone Dome. |
| Passive/gap-filler sensing                   | Twinvis passive radar, acoustic networks, passive RF systems.       |
| Russian SHORAD                               | Tor-M2E, Pantsir-S1, Podlet/Kasta/Nebo-type support radars.         |
| Chinese low-altitude/counter-drone ecosystem | JY-11, YLC-12, YLC-18, JH-18, AUICS-3000, 3G Mesh-type systems.     |
| UAE architecture                             | U.S. FS-LIDS procurement plus EDGE SkyShield.                       |

The most defensible conclusion from public evidence is that **Shahed-type detection is a sensor-fusion problem**. Long-range radars provide warning, short-range AESA radars provide low-altitude track quality, EO/IR and passive sensors reduce false positives, and C2 fusion determines whether the track becomes actionable.
