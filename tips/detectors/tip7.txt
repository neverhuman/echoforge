# Public-source technical report: military radar/sensor “detectors” for Shahed‑136/Geran‑2–class drones

**Scope note:** I’m treating “radar detectors” as **military detection sensors and sensor-fusion systems**: active radar, passive radar/RF, acoustic arrays, EO/IR, and command-and-control fusion. True military radar “sensitivity” values—minimum detectable signal, receiver noise figure, thresholds, proprietary waveforms, classified ECCM, exact engagement geometry—are usually not public. Where sensitivity is not published, the best public proxies are **frequency band, radar type, range against representative targets, update rate, angular/range accuracy, track capacity, false-alarm controls, and stated target class**.

The short version is: **Shahed‑136/Geran‑2–class one-way attack drones are usually detected by layered systems**, not one magic radar. A practical stack uses **medium/long-range air-defense radars for area warning**, **short-range C‑UAS radars for low/slow/small classification**, **acoustic arrays for cheap wide-area cueing**, **EO/IR for visual ID**, and **C2 fusion systems** such as U.S. FAAD/LIDS or Ukraine’s Sky Map/Sky Fortress-style networks.

---

## 1. Why Shahed‑class drones are hard radar targets

A Shahed‑136/Geran‑2–type target is a **fixed-wing, one-way attack UAV/loitering munition** flying at low altitude with a relatively small radar signature compared with crewed aircraft. Robin Radar’s Ukraine-driven IRIS Long Range Mode material explicitly describes detecting and classifying **Shahed loitering munitions and fixed-wing drones**, and notes Shahed threats traveling above **180 km/h** in the Ukrainian context. ([Robin Radar][1])

The hard parts are not mysterious:

* **Small radar cross-section:** NATO’s Joint Air Power Competence Centre notes that drones can have low radar reflectivity because of small size, plastic/composite construction, and low-altitude operation, making them challenging for legacy surveillance radars. ([japcc.org][2])
* **Low altitude:** low-flying targets are constrained by radar horizon, terrain masking, ground clutter, buildings, trees, sea/land multipath, and atmospheric refraction. MIT Lincoln Laboratory’s radar-propagation material describes how refraction, ducting, multipath, and attenuation can change whether a target is seen or missed. ([MIT Lincoln Laboratory][3])
* **Bird/aircraft clutter:** small UAVs and birds can have comparable size, speed, and radar cross-section, which is why micro-Doppler, track behavior, and multi-sensor fusion matter. ([UCL Discovery][4])
* **False alarms vs missed detections:** lowering thresholds increases detections but also false alarms. MIT’s radar detection notes tie probability of detection to target cross section, propagation, target fluctuation, receiver noise, threshold choice, false-alarm probability, SNR, and range. ([MIT Lincoln Laboratory][5])

## 2. Radar science: how these sensors actually detect drones

### 2.1 The radar equation and why small drones are punishing

The basic radar problem is: transmit energy, wait for a tiny reflection, then decide whether the return is target or noise. MIT Lincoln Laboratory’s radar equation material gives the core received-SNR relationship: transmitted power, antenna gain, wavelength, target radar cross section, thermal noise, bandwidth, system temperature, losses, and range all matter; the target return falls with the **fourth power of range** in monostatic radar. ([MIT Lincoln Laboratory][5])

A simplified monostatic form is:

[
SNR \propto \frac{P_t G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma}{R^4 k T_s B_n L}
]

Where:

* (P_t): transmit power
* (G): antenna gain
* (\lambda): wavelength
* (\sigma): radar cross-section
* (R): range
* (kT_sB_n): thermal noise power
* (L): losses

The brutal result: **if target radar cross-section drops by 16×, detection range only drops by about 2×**, because range scales roughly with the fourth root of RCS. That fourth-root relationship is why manufacturers often publish different detection ranges for “nano UAV,” “medium UAV,” “fighter,” “helicopter,” and “vehicle” targets rather than one universal range.

### 2.2 “Sensitivity” in public specs

In classified or engineering documentation, sensitivity might mean **minimum detectable signal**, **noise figure**, **receiver dynamic range**, **detection threshold**, **minimum SNR for a given probability of detection and false-alarm rate**, or **range against a target of known RCS**. Public C‑UAS datasheets almost never publish all of that.

Public proxies include:

* **Range against representative UAV classes:** e.g., DRS RADA MHR claims nano UAV detection at 5 km and medium UAV at 25 km; the larger exMHR claims nano UAV at 18 km and medium UAV at 80 km. ([DRS RADA Technologies][6])
* **Track update rate:** Echodyne states 10 Hz update rates for EchoShield/EchoGuard-class radars, and its buyer’s guide warns that update rates below about 8 Hz can struggle for fast drone intercept/camera cueing use cases. ([Echodyne][7])
* **Angular/range accuracy:** Echodyne publishes sub‑0.5° angular accuracy for EchoShield-class systems and range-resolution figures in the meter scale depending on waveform. ([Echodyne][7])
* **False-alarm controls:** Blighter publishes adjustable false-alarm-rate behavior and AI/micro-Doppler false-alarm reduction for the A800 Mk2. ([Blighter][8])
* **Classification features:** HENSOLDT SPEXER, Robin IRIS, Blighter A800, and RADA MHR all emphasize Doppler/micro-Doppler, clutter suppression, or classification features for distinguishing UAVs from birds, ground clutter, and other targets. ([HENSOLDT][9])

### 2.3 Doppler and micro-Doppler

**Doppler radar** measures velocity by the frequency shift of the return. This lets radars reject static clutter and separate moving drones from background. **Micro-Doppler** looks at small motion components: rotor blades, propellers, wingbeats, vibration, or body motion. For drone detection, micro-Doppler is especially useful for separating drones from birds and other small movers. Academic radar work explicitly identifies the bird-versus-drone problem as difficult because birds can be similar in size, speed, and RCS to small UAVs. ([UCL Discovery][4])

That is why C‑UAS radars advertise “micro-Doppler” heavily. Robin IRIS says it distinguishes drones from birds using blade/rotating-part signatures; HENSOLDT SPEXER describes micro-Doppler classification; Blighter A800 advertises AI-based micro-Doppler false-alarm reduction. ([Robin Radar][10])

### 2.4 Frequency bands: what changes from UHF/L/S/X/Ku/K/mmWave

There is no universally best band:

| Band                   |                                                      Typical role | Why used                                        | Drone-detection tradeoff                                                                         |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------: | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| UHF / VHF / meter-wave | Long-range early warning, counter-stealth, wide-area surveillance | Longer wavelength, large coverage               | Lower angular resolution unless antennas are large; not ideal alone for small-UAS classification |
| L-band                 |                       Long-range surveillance, air-defense search | Good area coverage and weather tolerance        | Less fine detail than X/Ku; useful as outer layer                                                |
| S-band                 |                                Air-defense / multi-mission radars | Balance of range, resolution, weather           | Common in mobile air-defense and multi-mission radars                                            |
| C/X-band               |   Fire-control, short/medium surveillance, counter-battery, C‑UAS | Better angular resolution with compact antennas | More weather/attenuation concerns than lower bands; often strong for low/small targets           |
| Ku/K/mmWave            |     Dedicated C‑UAS, precision tracking, short-range fire-control | High resolution, compact AESA/FMCW designs      | Shorter range and greater attenuation; excellent for classification and cueing                   |

Examples: Raytheon KuRFS is **Ku-band AESA**; Sentinel is **X-band**; RADA MHR/exMHR are **S-band AESA**; Blighter A800 is **Ku-band FMCW/Doppler AESA**; EchoGuard uses **K-band**; TRML‑4D is an AESA air-defense radar; SPEXER is **X-band pulse-Doppler AESA**. ([RTX][11])

## 3. Publicly documented sensor types used against Shahed‑class threats

### 3.1 Active radar

This is the primary military detection layer. Relevant families include:

* **3D air-surveillance radars**: detect range, azimuth, elevation.
* **4D radars**: usually range, azimuth, elevation, Doppler/radial velocity; sometimes also richer target features.
* **Pulse-Doppler radars**: reject stationary clutter and measure velocity.
* **FMCW radars**: continuous-wave chirp systems that can provide accurate range/velocity with lower power, common in C‑UAS.
* **AESA radars**: electronically steered arrays; fast beam scheduling, multi-target tracking, multiple simultaneous functions.
* **Rotating radars vs fixed-panel radars**: rotating systems provide 360° coverage with revisit limits; multi-panel AESA systems can provide high update rates and persistent hemispheric coverage.

### 3.2 Acoustic arrays

Ukraine’s Sky Fortress is the standout public example. U.S. Army CALL’s Ukraine report says the network used about **9,500 acoustic sensors**, each roughly **$400–$500**, to detect and pinpoint one-way UAVs; the system sent compact detections rather than raw audio and used bearings/time stamps for triangulation. 

Reuters later reported that Ukraine’s broader Sky Map/Sky Fortress-style ecosystem used more than **10,000 acoustic sensors**, fusing data from radars and other sensors, and that Ukrainian Sky Map was being deployed at Prince Sultan Air Base. ([Reuters][12])

Acoustics are attractive against Shahed-class drones because they are cheap, passive, and scalable, but they are also weather/noise/terrain dependent and generally need fusion with radar/EO/air-defense C2.

### 3.3 Passive RF / COMINT / direction finding

Passive RF sensors detect control links, telemetry, video downlinks, GNSS use, or other emissions. They are excellent against commercial quadcopters and many tactical UAVs, but less reliable against autonomous one-way attack drones that may not use a continuous control link. Rafael’s Drone Dome includes passive RF direction-finding/DTOA sensors for detecting, classifying, identifying, and locating drones/operators; EDGE/SIGN4L’s UAE SKYSHIELD also includes direction finders and C2/effectors in a networked C‑UAS architecture. ([Rafael][13])

### 3.4 EO/IR

Electro-optical/infrared sensors are usually **confirmatory** rather than primary wide-area detection. They are excellent for ID and fire-control cueing but limited by field of view, weather, haze, background, and line of sight. Rafael’s Drone Dome publishes EO/IR identification/recognition/detection roles out to kilometer-scale ranges when cued by its sensor suite. ([Rafael][13])

### 3.5 Passive radar / multistatic radar

Passive radar uses illuminators of opportunity—broadcast TV, radio, cellular, or other emitters—instead of transmitting itself. Leonardo’s AULOS passive radar is advertised for air-target detection using external illumination, with micro-UAV detection beyond 7 km in public marketing material. ([Leonardo Electronics][14])

China’s public radar-industrial base includes several passive systems: CASI’s 2025 report describes JY‑50 using civilian mobile phone/radio/TV emissions, YLC‑29 using civilian FM radio, and DWL002 as a passive emitter-locating system. ([Air University][15])

## 4.1 U.S. and allied C‑UAS / low-altitude systems

### Raytheon KuRFS / U.S. Army LIDS

**KuRFS** is one of the most important public U.S. C‑UAS radar references. RTX describes it as a **Ku-band 360° radar** for persistent detection, identification, and tracking of rockets, artillery, mortars, and all drone classes; RTX also says it is paired with Coyote interceptors in the U.S. Army’s Low, slow, small UAS Integrated Defeat System, or **LIDS**. ([RTX][11])

RTX’s public KuRFS material emphasizes **AESA**, **Ku-band high resolution**, discrimination of small objects/biologicals, and enough sensitivity to detect a **9 mm bullet**. ([RTX][11]) RTX describes LIDS as integrating **KuRFS**, **Coyote**, Northrop Grumman **FAAD C2**, and SRC electronic warfare elements in fixed, relocatable, and mobile configurations. ([RTX][16])

SRC’s **AN/TPQ‑50** is a multi-mission radar used for C‑UAS, early warning, counterfire, and SHORAD. Public SRC specs include **L-band**, **360° surveillance**, **3D target location**, **pulsed-Doppler operation**, **automatic detection/tracking/discrimination/geolocation**, **>35 km instrumented range**, and detection of traditional aircraft as well as low-altitude, slow, small-RCS targets such as ultralights, gliders, and UAS. # 5. Country-by-country: what the U.S., UAE, China, Russia, Ukraine, and others publicly use

## 5.1 United States

The U.S. public C‑UAS architecture is heavily layered.

### Core U.S. C‑UAS stack

The most visible Army system is **LIDS**: Raytheon KuRFS radar, Coyote interceptors, Northrop Grumman FAAD C2, and SRC electronic warfare elements. RTX says LIDS detects, tracks, identifies, and defeats Group 1–3 drones in fixed/relocatable and mobile configurations. ([RTX][16])

**KuRFS** is the key public radar: 360° Ku-band AESA, C‑RAM and C‑UAS roles, all drone classes, biological/small-object discrimination, and high-resolution operation. ([RTX][11])

### U.S. area/SHORAD radars

The U.S. also uses **Sentinel** radars for SHORAD and NASAMS-type networks. Sentinel provides 3D X-band air surveillance and fire-control cueing against drones, cruise missiles, aircraft, and helicopters. ([Kongsberg][18]) Sentinel A4 extends the mission set to RAM, cruise missiles, UAS, fixed-wing and rotary-wing aircraft with advanced AESA multi-mission operation. ([Dote][20])

### U.S. public sensor examples

| Layer                 | U.S. example                       | Role against Shahed-class target                                     |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Wide-area air defense | Sentinel / Sentinel A4             | Detect and track low/mid-altitude air threats; feed NASAMS/SHORAD C2 |
| Dedicated C‑UAS       | KuRFS / KuMRFS / LIDS              | Persistent low/slow/small detection and tracking                     |
| C2 fusion             | FAAD C2 / LIDS                     | Combine radar, EW, EO, and interceptors                              |
| Defeat layer          | Coyote, guns, missiles, lasers, EW | Not detection, but tied to sensor cueing                             |

Sources: RTX, SRC, Kongsberg, Raytheon, DOT&E. ([RTX][11])

## 5.2 Ukraine

Ukraine is the most important public case study for Shahed‑136/Geran‑2 detection because of the scale of attacks.

### Acoustic wide-area detection: Sky Fortress / Sky Map

U.S. Army CALL reports that Ukraine’s **Sky Fortress** used about **9,500 acoustic sensors** to detect and pinpoint one-way UAVs, sending compact detections rather than raw audio, triangulating tracks using bearing/time data, and cueing mobile fire teams. 

Reuters reports that Ukraine’s **Sky Map** ecosystem synthesizes data from radars and other sensors, and that Sky Fortress had more than **10,000 acoustic sensors** across Ukraine. Reuters also reports that the system has been deployed at Prince Sultan Air Base. ([Reuters][12])

### Western radars supplied or used in Ukraine

Ukraine publicly receives or uses several Western radar families relevant to drone/cruise-missile defense:

* **HENSOLDT TRML‑4D** and **SPEXER 2000 3D MkIII**, explicitly announced for Ukraine. ([HENSOLDT][25])
* **Sentinel/NASAMS-associated radars**, with Sentinel providing X-band 3D surveillance against UAS and cruise missiles. ([Kongsberg][18])
* **Robin IRIS Long Range Mode**, developed and stress-tested through Ukraine and explicitly marketed for Shahed loitering munitions. ([Robin Radar][1])
* **Other NATO C‑UAS/SHORAD radars** are likely integrated into a fused air-defense picture, but public data rarely reveals exact placement, coverage, or radar-to-effector pairing.

### What Ukraine’s approach demonstrates

Ukraine’s public approach shows why Shahed-class defense is not “just radar”:

| Function               | Public Ukrainian-style solution                              |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Cheap wide-area cueing | Acoustic nodes / Sky Fortress                                |
| Air picture            | Military radars and national air-defense network             |
| Point defense          | SHORAD, guns, interceptors, EW, mobile fire teams            |
| Fusion                 | Sky Map / C2 layer combining sensors                         |
| ID/cueing              | EO/IR, human reporting, radar tracks, acoustic triangulation |

The key lesson is that a low-cost passive sensor network can cue scarce high-end radars and interceptors, reducing the need to illuminate every square kilometer continuously with expensive active sensors.

## 5.3 United Arab Emirates

The UAE appears publicly to use a **layered national and point-defense model**: high-end air/missile-defense radars for ballistic/cruise/air threats, plus dedicated C‑UAS systems for local drone defense.

### EDGE / SIGN4L SKYSHIELD

EDGE Group’s **SKYSHIELD** is a UAE C‑UAS system. EDGE describes it as a trailer-mounted C‑UAS solution with detection/identification, radar and EO/IR components, RF direction finding, jamming/spoofing, and C2 integration. Public SKYSHIELD details include 400–6000 MHz RF coverage for jammer/spoofer elements, GNSS jamming/spoofing, radar/EO as OEM-dependent components, and C2/networking. ([EDGE][30])

SIGN4L describes the broader SKYSHIELD architecture as networked sensors, 3D radars, EO cameras, direction finders, and effectors integrated into EDGE’s unified C2 for automated 360° detection and defeat, deployable as fixed, vehicle, truck, or naval configurations. ([SIGN4L][31])

### Patriot / THAAD layer

Raytheon describes **Global Patriot** as using radars, C2, and interceptors to detect, identify, and defeat tactical ballistic missiles, cruise missiles, drones, and advanced aircraft. ([RTX][32]) Public DSCA-related records identify UAE-funded Patriot AN/MPQ‑65 radar variants, indicating Patriot radar relevance in the UAE context. ([samm.dsca.mil][33])

# 7. What actually works best against Shahed‑136/Geran‑2–class drones?

## 7.1 Best public architecture

A strong public-source architecture looks like this:

1. **Outer air-defense radar layer**
   TRML‑4D, Sentinel, Giraffe 4A, Patriot-type radars, larger national air-defense radars. These give wide-area air picture, but low-altitude terrain masking and small-RCS limits still matter.

2. **Low-altitude / C‑UAS radar layer**
   KuRFS, KuMRFS, RADA MHR/exMHR, SPEXER, Giraffe 1X, Robin IRIS, Blighter A800, EchoShield, Drone Dome radars. These are designed for low/slow/small targets and classification.

3. **Passive wide-area cueing**
   Acoustic arrays like Ukraine’s Sky Fortress and passive RF/radar where applicable. Acoustic arrays are especially useful when targets are noisy and numerous, and when radars/interceptors are scarce. 

4. **EO/IR confirmation**
   Cameras verify target class, reduce false positives, and cue interceptors/guns/lasers.

5. **C2 fusion**
   FAAD/LIDS, Sky Map-like platforms, or national air-defense C2 systems correlate tracks, prevent duplicate engagements, and route targets to effectors.

6. **Defeat layer**
   Guns, missiles, interceptors, directed energy, and EW. Detection alone does not solve the problem; the sensor must produce timely, high-confidence tracks.

## 7.2 Which sensor is “best”?

There is no single best detector. Public evidence suggests the best choice depends on the mission:

| Mission                                   | Best-fit sensor family                                                      |
| ----------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| National/regional warning                 | TRML‑4D, Sentinel, Giraffe 4A, larger L/S-band radars                       |
| Point defense of a base                   | KuRFS/LIDS, RADA MHR/exMHR, Drone Dome, SKYSHIELD, SPEXER                   |
| Cheap wide-area Shahed cueing             | Acoustic arrays + fusion, like Sky Fortress/Sky Map                         |
| Mobile SHORAD                             | KuRFS mobile LIDS, Giraffe 1X, RADA MHR, SPEXER, Sentinel                   |
| Dense urban drone detection               | Short-range AESA/FMCW radar + EO/IR + RF sensors                            |
| Bird/drone discrimination                 | Micro-Doppler radars: Robin, Blighter, SPEXER, RADA, Echodyne-class systems |
| Autonomous one-way drones with no RF link | Active radar + acoustic + EO/IR; passive RF is less reliable                |

## 7.3 What people use specifically for Shahed-like threats

Based on public sources:

* **Ukraine:** acoustic Sky Fortress/Sky Map, air-defense radar networks, TRML‑4D/SPEXER, Sentinel/NASAMS-type radars, IRIS Long Range Mode, and other NATO-supplied C‑UAS/SHORAD sensors. 
* **United States:** LIDS with KuRFS, Coyote, FAAD C2, SRC EW; Sentinel/Sentinel A4; other C‑UAS radars such as KuMRFS and RADA-class systems depending on unit/program. ([RTX][11])
* **UAE:** EDGE/SIGN4L SKYSHIELD for point C‑UAS; Patriot/THAAD layers for broader air/missile defense, though THAAD’s AN/TPY‑2 is not a small-drone radar. ([EDGE][30])
* **China:** YLC‑48, S-band 3D low-altitude radar, YLC‑15/YLC‑18, passive radar systems, and larger L/UHF early-warning networks. ([Air University][15])
* **Russia:** Pantsir/Tor-style SHORAD radars and EO, broader radars like Gamma‑S1TE, plus EW systems such as Repellent/Kupol/Rubezh/Silok-type families. ([Rostec Export][36])
* **Israel/Europe:** Drone Dome, Drone Guard, HENSOLDT TRML/SPEXER, Saab Giraffe, Blighter, Robin, Leonardo DRS RADA, Echodyne-class compact radars. ([Rafael][13])

# 8. Low-elevation radar issues and false positives

## 8.1 Radar horizon and terrain

Low-altitude detection is line-of-sight limited. Terrain, buildings, vegetation, and earth curvature reduce detection range for low flyers. Raising a radar or networking multiple radars helps, but exact siting is operationally sensitive and environment-specific.

## 8.2 Ground clutter

Low-flying drones appear against the ground, not clean sky. Pulse-Doppler filtering helps reject stationary clutter, but moving ground vehicles, wind-blown trees, birds, sea clutter, rain, and urban multipath can generate false tracks. MIT’s radar-propagation material explicitly discusses multipath/reflection/refraction and how targets may be seen or not seen depending on propagation. ([MIT Lincoln Laboratory][3])

## 8.3 Birds

Birds are among the biggest false-positive classes. Research on radar bird/drone discrimination notes that birds and small UAVs can be similar in size, velocity, and radar cross-section, making misclassification a real problem. ([UCL Discovery][4]) That is why commercial/military C‑UAS radars emphasize micro-Doppler and classification algorithms.

## 8.4 Weather

Rain, fog, humidity, dust, and ducting can alter radar propagation and clutter. MIT’s propagation notes show that atmospheric attenuation, refraction, ducting, and multipath are material radar-performance factors. ([MIT Lincoln Laboratory][3]) Higher-frequency radars often provide better resolution but can suffer more attenuation or weather sensitivity than lower-frequency surveillance radars.

## 8.5 Track confirmation delay

A radar may detect a return before it confirms a track. Modern systems use track-before-detect, multi-frame confirmation, CFAR thresholds, and classification logic. The tradeoff is speed versus false alarms. Echodyne’s buyer’s guide emphasizes update rate and track quality for drone defense, and recommends high update rates for slewing cameras and effectors. ([Echodyne][40])

# 9. Practical public conclusions

1. **The strongest Shahed-detection approach is layered.** Medium/long-range radars provide area warning; C‑UAS radars provide low-altitude classification; acoustic arrays provide cheap passive cueing; EO/IR confirms; C2 fuses tracks.

2. **Dedicated C‑UAS radars matter because legacy air-surveillance radars were not optimized for low/slow/small targets.** JAPCC explicitly notes the low reflectivity and low-altitude challenge for drones. ([japcc.org][2])

3. **For Shahed-like fixed-wing threats, acoustic networks are unusually important.** Ukraine’s Sky Fortress/Sky Map public record shows thousands of low-cost sensors providing wide-area cueing against one-way UAVs. 

4. **Micro-Doppler and classification are crucial for false-alarm control.** Bird/drone confusion is a known radar problem, and leading C‑UAS systems advertise micro-Doppler or AI classification features. ([UCL Discovery][4])

5. **Published detection ranges are not universal.** They depend on radar cross-section, aspect, altitude, clutter, environment, target speed, radar update rate, classification threshold, and required probability of detection/false alarm.

6. **Country differences are mostly architectural.** The U.S. emphasizes LIDS/KuRFS/FAAD-style integrated C‑UAS; Ukraine has pioneered mass acoustic cueing and sensor fusion under real Shahed attack conditions; UAE markets SKYSHIELD and fields high-end air/missile defense; China has dedicated YLC‑48-style anti-drone radars plus broader low-altitude networks; Russia combines Pantsir/Tor-style SHORAD with EW and broader air-defense radars.

7. **The most public, spec-rich Shahed-relevant systems are Robin IRIS LRM, RADA MHR/exMHR, Blighter A800, HENSOLDT SPEXER/TRML‑4D, Raytheon KuRFS/LIDS, Saab Giraffe, Rafael Drone Dome, and Ukraine’s Sky Fortress/Sky Map.** These provide the clearest public evidence for how modern forces detect low-altitude one-way attack drones.
