# Public-source report: military radar and sensor systems used to detect Shahed‑136–class drones and other low/slow/small air threats

I’m using **“radar detectors”** here to mean **military radar and sensor systems that detect drones, loitering munitions, cruise missiles, aircraft, rockets/artillery/mortars, and low-altitude threats**. I am **not** using it in the consumer “police radar detector” sense, and I am not treating this as a guide for defeating radar. Where exact receiver sensitivity, detection thresholds, electronic-protection parameters, classified waveforms, or deployment geometry are not public, I label them as **not publicly specified** and use the best public proxy: **band, target class, instrumented range, published detection/tracking range, angular/range accuracy, update rate, track capacity, and stated target types**.

---

## 1. Executive takeaways

**Shahed‑136 / Geran‑2–class one-way attack UAVs are difficult but not exotic targets.** They are slow, low-altitude, propeller-driven, and relatively small compared with aircraft, but larger and often louder than micro‑UAS quadcopters. Public OSINT visual guides list the Shahed‑136 at roughly **3.5 m length and 2.5 m wingspan**; one OSINT radar-cross-section estimate places its X-band RCS around **0.01–0.05 m²**, but that estimate should be treated as approximate because RCS changes strongly with aspect angle, payload, surface finish, and frequency. ([Open Source Munitions Portal][1])

The main detection problem is not “radar cannot see it.” The real problem is **low altitude + clutter + slow speed + small/variable RCS + saturation + line of sight**. A U.S. Army CALL report on Ukraine’s low-altitude air-defense problem explicitly notes that small UAS and loitering munitions have **low radar cross section, low altitude, and slow speeds**, which complicate legacy radar detection and tracking. It also describes Ukraine’s use of distributed acoustic sensors to detect engine/propeller signatures and cue other defenses. ([Army API][2])

The best public model for a modern C‑UAS detection stack is **layered sensing**, not a single radar. The U.S. Army’s **LIDS / FS‑LIDS** architecture publicly combines **Ku-band radar, AN/TPQ‑50 radar, passive RF/electronic-warfare sensors, EO/IR cameras, FAAD C2, and kinetic/non-kinetic effectors**. RTX says its KuRFS radar is paired with Coyote interceptors in the Army LIDS system, while SRC’s LIDS material describes detection/tracking via AN/TPQ‑50 and Ku-band multifunction RF systems, passive RF direction finding, EO/IR identification, FAAD C2, EW, Coyote, and 30 mm defeat layers. ([RTX][3])

For Shahed‑type threats specifically, the best public evidence points to **four complementary detection layers**:
**1) medium/long-range air-defense radar** such as TRML‑4D, Giraffe 4A, GM200 MM/C, ELTA Green Rock/MS‑MMR, Sentinel/NASAMS, or similar systems;
**2) high-resolution short-range C‑UAS radar** such as KuRFS/KuMRFS, SPEXER, RADA/Leonardo MHR, Blighter A400, Echodyne EchoShield/EchoGuard, Saab Giraffe 1X, Robin IRIS, or comparable systems;
**3) passive acoustic/RF sensing** where available, especially for engine-noisy one-way attack drones; and
**4) EO/IR confirmation and classification** before engagement. Ukraine’s “Sky Fortress,” “Zvook,” and “FENEK” acoustic networks are public examples of this low-cost cueing layer. ([Army API][2])

## 2. Why Shahed‑136–type drones are a hard radar problem

### Physical target features

A Shahed‑136 is not stealth in the fifth-generation-aircraft sense. It is a **low-cost, propeller-driven, delta-wing loitering munition / one-way attack UAV**. Public OSINT dimensions put the Shahed‑136 at about **3.5 m long with a 2.5 m wingspan**, while the smaller Shahed‑131 is about **2.6 m long with a 2.2 m wingspan**. ([Open Source Munitions Portal][1])

The radar challenge comes from the combination of:

| Factor                           | Why it matters for detection                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Low altitude**                 | Ground-based radar line of sight is limited by terrain, buildings, vegetation, earth curvature, and radar height. Low-altitude targets also sit inside ground clutter, multipath, and weather clutter.                                                                                                   |
| **Small/variable RCS**           | Public estimates put Shahed‑136 X-band RCS around **0.01–0.05 m²**, but RCS is aspect- and frequency-dependent. A small RCS lowers signal-to-noise ratio and track confidence. ([Drone Warfare][4])                                                                                                      |
| **Slow speed**                   | Slow radial velocity can overlap with birds, ground vehicles, wind-blown clutter, or hovering/low-Doppler returns, making classification harder.                                                                                                                                                         |
| **Propeller micro-Doppler**      | The propeller can help classify the target, but extracting that signature requires suitable radar waveform, dwell time, processing, and range/SNR. Blighter, Hensoldt SPEXER, and Robin Radar all explicitly advertise micro-Doppler / spectral classification for drone discrimination. ([Blighter][5]) |
| **Autonomy / weak RF signature** | Passive RF sensors work best when the drone emits or communicates. One-way attack drones may use preprogrammed navigation and may not provide a strong command link. Echodyne’s C‑UAS material notes that advanced UAS can have little or no communication to detect or jam. ([Trade.gov][6])            |
| **Mass attacks**                 | Shahed waves stress track capacity, identification, C2, interceptor inventory, and operator workload more than a single-drone test.                                                                                                                                                                      |

### Publicly stated low-altitude detection issue

The U.S. Army CALL report on Ukraine describes the core problem directly: low-RCS, low-altitude, slow UAS and loitering munitions complicate legacy radar; Ukraine therefore uses acoustic sensors that detect engine/propeller signatures, process them locally, triangulate, and cue other defenses. ([Army API][2])

## 3. Radar science: how these sensors actually work

### 3.1 Active radar basics

An active radar transmits electromagnetic energy, listens for reflections, and estimates target state from the return:

**Range** comes from time delay:
[
R = \frac{c \Delta t}{2}
]

**Radial velocity** comes from Doppler shift:
[
f_d = \frac{2v_r}{\lambda}
]

**Angle** comes from antenna pointing, monopulse comparison, phase differences, or digital beamforming.

The simplified monostatic radar equation is:

[
P_r =
\frac{P_t G_t G_r \lambda^2 \sigma}
{(4\pi)^3 R^4 L}
]

Where:

| Term       | Meaning                       |
| ---------- | ----------------------------- |
| (P_r)      | received echo power           |
| (P_t)      | transmitted power             |
| (G_t, G_r) | transmit/receive antenna gain |
| (\lambda)  | wavelength                    |
| (\sigma)   | target radar cross section    |
| (R)        | target range                  |
| (L)        | system and propagation losses |

The key operational result is the **fourth-power range law**: received power falls with (R^4) for monostatic radar. Doubling range requires about 16× more favorable power/aperture/RCS/loss budget. MIT Lincoln Laboratory training material frames the radar equation as the relationship between target properties such as RCS, radar characteristics such as power and aperture, range, and propagation losses. ([MIT Lincoln Laboratory][7])

### 3.2 “Sensitivity” in military radar

People often ask for “sensitivity,” but for military radar this has several meanings:

| Sensitivity concept                    | What it means                                                                                                                               | Why public data is rare                                                    |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Receiver sensitivity / noise floor** | Minimum signal at receiver input, usually tied to (kTB), noise figure, bandwidth, and required SNR.                                         | Often classified/proprietary because it exposes real detection capability. |
| **Detection sensitivity**              | Probability of detecting a target of given RCS at a given range, clutter, aspect, and false-alarm rate.                                     | True curves are usually classified or test-range proprietary.              |
| **Tracking sensitivity**               | Ability to maintain track after detection; often more important than first detection.                                                       | Vendors may publish “track range” but not full Pd/Pfa curves.              |
| **Classification sensitivity**         | Ability to distinguish drone/bird/aircraft/projectile using Doppler, micro-Doppler, RCS, track behavior, EO/IR, RF, or acoustic signatures. | Algorithms and training data are usually proprietary.                      |
| **False-alarm control**                | Ability to suppress birds, weather, terrain, multipath, and clutter.                                                                        | Real performance depends on environment and deployment.                    |

Because receiver sensitivity is rarely public, the best public proxies are **published detection/tracking range by target class**, **RCS-specific range**, **track capacity**, **update rate**, **angular/range accuracy**, **instrumented range**, and **stated clutter/false-alarm features**.

A good example is **Blighter A400**, which publishes RCS-specific maximum detection ranges: **0.01 m² at 2.73 km, 0.05 m² at 4.09 km, 0.1 m² at 4.87 km, 0.5 m² at 7.28 km, 1.0 m² at 8.66 km, and 10 m² at 15.40 km**, with adjustable false-alarm rate and more than 500 tracks per scan. That is far more useful publicly than a bare “receiver sensitivity” number. ([Blighter][5])

### 3.3 Frequency band tradeoffs

| Band family        | Typical role                                                                              | Drone/Shahed relevance                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **VHF/UHF/L-band** | Long-range surveillance, volume search, better propagation, less fine resolution.         | Useful for early warning and air picture; less precise for small-target classification. AN/TPQ‑50 is publicly specified as **L-band** with air-surveillance instrumented range over **35 km**. ([SRC][8])                              |
| **S/C/G-band**     | Multi-mission GBAD radars, air surveillance, weapon cueing, C‑RAM, SHORAD.                | Common compromise between range, resolution, weather robustness, and cost. Examples include Leonardo/RADA MHR S-band, ELTA Green Rock S-band, and TRML‑4D in NATO G-band/C-band terminology. ([DRS RADA Technologies][9])              |
| **X/Ku/K-band**    | High-resolution short-to-medium range search, drone discrimination, fire-control support. | Excellent for small drones and micro-Doppler, but more line-of-sight and weather-sensitive than lower bands. KuRFS, Blighter A400, Hensoldt SPEXER, Echodyne, and Sentinel-type systems sit in this high-resolution family. ([RTX][3]) |
| **Passive radar**  | Uses external illuminators such as FM/DAB/DVB-T instead of transmitting.                  | Attractive for covert surveillance and gap filling; performance depends on illuminator geometry and signal environment. Hensoldt Twinvis uses VHF/UHF broadcast reflections and provides 360° passive 3D tracking. ([HENSOLDT][10])    |
| **Acoustic**       | Passive microphones and arrays detect engine/propeller sound.                             | Very relevant for Shahed-type piston/propeller drones; short-range/local and weather/noise dependent. Ukraine’s acoustic networks are a major public example. ([Army API][2])                                                          |

### 3.4 Signal processing used against drones

Modern C‑UAS radars generally combine:

**Pulse-Doppler processing.** Separates moving targets from stationary clutter using Doppler shift. This is foundational for finding airborne targets near the ground.

**CFAR thresholding.** Constant False Alarm Rate processing adapts detection thresholds to local noise/clutter so the radar does not flood operators with false tracks. MIT Lincoln Laboratory material on radar processing discusses Doppler filter banks, CFAR, and adaptive thresholds for limiting false alarms in noise/weather/clutter. ([MIT Lincoln Laboratory][11])

**AESA / phased-array beam steering.** Active electronically scanned arrays rapidly steer beams without mechanically rotating an antenna. This supports fast revisit, multi-beam operation, track-while-scan, and sector prioritization. KuRFS, TRML‑4D, Saab Giraffe 4A, ELTA Green Rock, Leonardo/RADA MHR, and SPEXER all advertise AESA-type architectures. ([RTX][3])

**Micro-Doppler classification.** Propellers and rotors create high-frequency modulation on the radar return. Vendors such as Blighter, Hensoldt SPEXER, Robin Radar, and Leonardo/RADA describe micro-Doppler, spectral classification, or 4D Doppler analysis to distinguish drones from birds and clutter. ([Blighter][5])

**Track-before-detect and multi-sensor fusion.** A weak single return may not be enough, but repeated returns across time plus cues from acoustic/RF/EO/IR can create a confident track. LIDS and Hensoldt’s Elysion/TwinSens concepts explicitly reflect this multi-sensor approach.

## 5. How a military detection chain works against a Shahed‑class attack

A realistic defensive chain looks like this:

### Step 1 — Wide-area warning

Longer-range GBAD or air-surveillance radars provide the broad air picture. Examples include **TRML‑4D, Giraffe 4A, GM200 MM/C, Green Rock/MS‑MMR, Sentinel/NASAMS, and similar radars**. These systems are designed to see aircraft, helicopters, cruise missiles, UAVs, and in some cases RAM, often with high track capacity and integration into air-defense C2. ([HENSOLDT][14])

### Step 2 — Low-altitude gap filling

Low-flying drones may be hidden by terrain, urban clutter, vegetation, or radar horizon from one sensor location. Gap filling can use shorter-range radars, passive radar, acoustic nodes, and RF direction-finding. Ukraine’s acoustic networks are a public example: distributed microphones detect engine/propeller signatures, run edge ML, triangulate/cue, and feed C2 or mobile teams. ([Army API][2])

### Step 3 — High-resolution C‑UAS tracking

Once a track is cued, high-resolution C‑UAS radars maintain a better track and help classify it. Ku/X/S-band systems like **KuRFS, SPEXER, MHR, Blighter A400, EchoShield, Green Rock, and Giraffe 1X** publish features aimed at low/slow/small targets: micro-Doppler, subclutter visibility, fast update, high track counts, drone/bird discrimination, or RCS-specific range. ([RTX][3])

### Step 4 — Identification

Radar alone may not be enough for positive identification, especially near civilian aircraft, birds, or friendly drones. EO/IR, passive RF, acoustic classification, IFF, and track behavior all help. LIDS/FS‑LIDS explicitly includes EO/IR for verification/identification/classification and passive RF direction-finding for detection/identification where RF emissions exist. 

### Step 5 — C2 and engagement

The detection layer must hand off to C2 and an effector: interceptor, missile, gun, EW, high-energy laser, or other approved defense. RTX and SRC describe LIDS as KuRFS/KuMRFS + Coyote + FAAD C2 + EW + guns/other effectors; the UAE FS‑LIDS notice similarly describes kinetic and electromagnetic-warfare defeat options. ([RTX][33])

## 6. False positives and low-elevation noise sources

The common false-positive / false-track sources for Shahed-class detection include:

| Source                                  | Why it confuses radar/sensors                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| --------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Birds and bird flocks**               | Similar size/RCS to small UAVs; slow/medium speeds; micro-Doppler from wings can mimic small moving targets. Vendors specifically advertise bird/drone discrimination to address this. ([Start][24])                   |
| **Ground vehicles**                     | At low elevation, moving vehicles can enter sidelobes or multipath returns and create Doppler signatures.                                                                                                              |
| **Wind turbines / rotating machinery**  | Strong micro-Doppler and clutter; can create persistent false alarms unless mapped or filtered.                                                                                                                        |
| **Weather**                             | Rain, hail, snow, insects, and atmospheric ducting can affect returns, especially at higher frequencies.                                                                                                               |
| **Urban multipath**                     | Reflections from buildings create ghost tracks and angle/range ambiguity.                                                                                                                                              |
| **Terrain and vegetation**              | Hills, trees, and foliage mask low-altitude targets and generate clutter.                                                                                                                                              |
| **Acoustic noise**                      | Roads, generators, machinery, weather, and battlefield noise degrade acoustic systems. CALL specifically notes acoustic limitations from ambient noise, atmospheric absorption, wind, and temperature. ([Army API][2]) |
| **Friendly drones / civilian aircraft** | Requires C2, IFF, procedural control, and EO/IR/RF confirmation to avoid fratricide. SRC’s LIDS material explicitly mentions aircraft avoidance, fratricide prevention, and Link‑16 interoperability through FAAD C2.  |

This is why mature systems emphasize **classification**, not just detection. A radar that “detects everything” but cannot suppress birds, clutter, and friendly activity is operationally weak.

## 7. What different countries appear to use against Shahed‑type threats

### United States

The most public U.S. C‑UAS architecture is **LIDS / FS‑LIDS**. It uses **KuRFS/KuMRFS**, **AN/TPQ‑50**, passive RF/EW, EO/IR, FAAD C2, Coyote interceptors, and other effectors. RTX states KuRFS is a 360° Ku-band AESA-style radar for all drone classes and C‑RAM-type threats, while SRC’s LIDS documentation lays out the full sensor-to-effector architecture. ([RTX][3])

For broader air defense, the U.S. and partners also use **Sentinel / NASAMS**-type radars that can detect UAVs, cruise missiles, aircraft, and helicopters, though those are not tiny-drone-only sensors. ([RTX][12])

### Ukraine

Ukraine uses a mix of Western-supplied GBAD radars, air-defense systems, mobile fire teams, acoustic networks, EO/IR, and other sensors. Public examples include **TRML‑4D**, **SPEXER**, **NASAMS/Sentinel**, and distributed acoustic systems such as **Sky Fortress, Zvook, and FENEK**. Hensoldt states it has delivered or been contracted for additional TRML‑4D and SPEXER radars for Ukraine, while the U.S. Army CALL report describes Ukraine’s acoustic networks in unusually detailed public terms. ([HENSOLDT][15])

Ukraine’s lesson is especially important: against Shaheds, **cheap passive cueing at scale** can be as important as a smaller number of expensive radars. Acoustic sensors do not replace radar, but they can warn, cue, and narrow the search problem for mobile teams and point-defense weapons. ([Army API][2])

### UAE and Gulf states

The UAE’s public FS‑LIDS request is a major indicator of where Gulf C‑UAS defense is going: **fixed-site, layered, radar + RF + EO/IR + C2 + kinetic/EW defeat**. The U.S. notice lists KuMRFS radars, EO/IR, FAAD C2, RF detectors, and Coyote Block 2 interceptors. ([Federal Register][13])

The UAE also has operated **Pantsir‑S1**, and Qatar has similarly requested FS‑LIDS, suggesting regional interest in layered C‑UAS architectures rather than relying only on legacy SHORAD. ([Missile Threat][18])

### Russia

Russia uses layered air defense with systems such as **Pantsir**, **Tor**, and larger surveillance/gap-filler radars. Public CSIS data describes Pantsir and Tor as capable against UAVs and low-altitude threats, with organic search/track radars and EO/thermal channels. ([Missile Threat][18])

Russia’s challenge is the same physics and operations problem everyone faces: low-altitude targets require dense coverage, good C2, disciplined identification, and enough effectors to handle mass attacks.

### China

China publicly markets and displays multiple anti-drone and SHORAD sensor systems. CASI identifies **YLC‑48 / UAV Terminator** as a portable phased-array anti-drone radar with 360° coverage and multi-target tracking, and describes HQ‑17, Smarthunter, and Yitian families as part of China’s short-range air-defense / low-altitude detection ecosystem. ([Air University][23])

The open record is thinner than for U.S./European vendors, so Chinese sensitivity and real operational performance against Shahed-like targets should be treated as less publicly verifiable.

The strongest technical caution is that **instrumented range is not detection range**. Instrumented range is the maximum range scale the radar can represent. A radar with a 250 km or 400 km instrumented range will not necessarily detect a small low-altitude drone at that range. The meaningful number is target-specific detection/tracking range at a stated probability of detection and false-alarm rate, and those curves are usually not public.

## 9. Practical ranking of sensor types for Shahed‑136 detection

For a Shahed‑type one-way attack drone, public evidence supports this rough defensive value ranking:

| Sensor layer                     | Strength                                                                                   | Weakness                                                                               |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Medium/long-range GBAD radar** | Early warning, air picture, integration with missiles and C2.                              | Low-altitude line-of-sight gaps; small-RCS/clutter limitations; expensive.             |
| **High-resolution C‑UAS radar**  | Better low/slow/small tracking, micro-Doppler, drone/bird discrimination, local defense.   | Shorter range; still line-of-sight; can be saturated.                                  |
| **Acoustic network**             | Cheap, passive, scalable, very relevant to noisy propeller drones; can cue mobile teams.   | Shorter range, weather/noise dependent, less precise than radar; not sufficient alone. |
| **Passive RF detection**         | Excellent against drones with command/video/data links; passive and geolocating.           | Less useful against autonomous or emission-controlled one-way attack drones.           |
| **EO/IR**                        | Identification and classification; useful for operator confirmation and terminal tracking. | Weather, darkness, smoke, clutter, field-of-view and line-of-sight limits.             |
| **Passive radar**                | Silent surveillance, gap filling, resilience, can cue active sensors.                      | Depends on broadcast illuminators and geometry; lower control over waveform.           |

The right answer is **fusion**. Hensoldt’s C‑UAS material states that compact drones have low noise/heat/radio spectra and that a single sensor is insufficient; its Elysion concept combines active/passive radars, RF, optics, acoustics, and C2. That matches the U.S. LIDS and Ukrainian acoustic-network lessons. ([HENSOLDT][34])

## 10. Bottom-line answer: what people use to detect Shahed‑136–type drones

**United States:** LIDS / FS‑LIDS with **KuRFS/KuMRFS**, **AN/TPQ‑50**, passive RF/EW, EO/IR, FAAD C2, Coyote, guns, and other effectors; Sentinel/NASAMS and other GBAD radars for broader air-defense layers. ([RTX][3])

**Ukraine:** A wartime fusion of **TRML‑4D**, **SPEXER**, **NASAMS/Sentinel**, other Western/Soviet radars, EO/IR, mobile teams, and large-scale acoustic networks such as **Sky Fortress, Zvook, and FENEK**. ([HENSOLDT][15])

**UAE / Gulf:** Public UAE FS‑LIDS request includes **KuMRFS, FAAD C2, EO/IR, RF detector antennas, Coyote Block 2**, and EW/kinetic options; UAE also has Pantsir history, and Qatar has requested a similar FS‑LIDS architecture. ([Federal Register][13])

**Russia:** **Pantsir**, **Tor**, and low-altitude surveillance/gap-filler radars, integrated into broader air-defense networks. Public details show UAV engagement roles and organic radar/EO channels, but real-world effectiveness depends on integration, terrain, saturation, and crew proficiency. ([Missile Threat][18])

**China:** Publicly displayed and marketed systems include **YLC‑48 / UAV Terminator**, **HQ‑17-family SHORAD**, **Smarthunter**, and **Yitian**, with CASI documenting Chinese emphasis on portable phased-array anti-drone radar, networked short-range air defense, and low-altitude detection. ([Air University][23])

The defensible technical conclusion is: **Shahed‑136 detection is best handled by a layered, fused air-defense network combining medium-range radar, high-resolution C‑UAS radar, acoustic cueing, passive RF where useful, EO/IR confirmation, and fast C2.** The public systems with the most directly relevant published specs are **KuRFS/KuMRFS/LIDS, AN/TPQ‑50, TRML‑4D, SPEXER, Saab Giraffe 1X/4A, Blighter A400, Leonardo/RADA MHR, Echodyne EchoShield, ELTA Green Rock/MS‑MMR, Thales GM200 MM/C, Sentinel/NASAMS, Pantsir, Tor, and China’s YLC‑48/HQ‑17 ecosystem**.
